\begin{abstract}
Write $\Lat$ for the Moser lattice and $\theta_a$ for the rotation with
$\cos\theta_a = (2a-1)/(2a)$, so that $a$ is the squared radius at which
$\theta_a$ pairs a point with its own image. We prove a divisibility obstruction:
if the joint lattice $\Lat + \theta_a(\Lat)$ has rank eight and $4 \nmid a$, it
carries a $4$-colouring by a group homomorphism, so no subset of it is
5-chromatic at any size, and no 5-chromatic union $L \cup \theta_a(S)$ exists for
any $L, S \subseteq \Lat$. The obstruction decides \emph{every} rational $a$,
before any graph is built.

Its engine is a structural fact: $3\Lat$ is the maximal order of
$\Q(\sqrt{33}, i\sqrt3)$. From that, $\Lat$'s two integral geometric
$4$-colourings are the reductions modulo the two primes above $2$, and occupancy
of the shell at radius $\sqrt a$ becomes a relative-norm condition, which Hasse's
theorem solves in closed form: $\Lat$ has a point at squared radius $a = n/b$ in
lowest terms if and only if $b \in \{1,3,9\}$ and $v_p(n)$ is even at every prime
that splits in $\Q(\sqrt{33})$ and stays inert in $\Q(\sqrt{-3})$. The rotations
surviving both conditions have natural density zero.

Against that, constructions. Chaining a monochromatic-pair gadget of Parts' gives
a $5$-chromatic join at $a = 64n^2/9$ for every $n$, an infinite family that
includes the one integer spindle our filters cannot decide. Seven spindles are
verified to join, three of them---$\theta_{16}$, $\theta_{28}$,
$\theta_{36}$---by constructions for which we have found no precedent. A periodic
refinement kills eleven further integer candidates below $100$. Finally, any
5-chromatic $L \cup \theta_4(S)$ needs at least five cross edges, and exactly
$1920$ five-edge sets defeat every pairing of homomorphism colourings; whether one
is realised is open. The record of $509$ is untouched.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The Hadwiger--Nelson problem asks for $\chi(\R^2)$, the least number of colours
needed so that no two points at distance $1$ share one. De~Grey \cite{deGrey}
proved $\chi(\R^2) \ge 5$ in 2018 with a 1581-vertex unit-distance graph;
Polymath16, Heule \cite{Heule} and Parts \cite{Parts} drove the vertex count down
to $509$, where it has stood since 2020.

The record is built out of one lattice, and so is everything in this paper. Fix
the \emph{Moser lattice} $\Lat$, the subgroup of $(\C,+)$ generated by the $30$
unit vectors
\[
  \exp\!\big(i(k\cdot 60^\circ + j\,\theta_3/2)\big),
  \qquad 0 \le k < 6, \quad \lvert j\rvert \le 2,
  \qquad \theta_3 = \arccos\tfrac56,
\]
where $\theta_3 = \arccos\tfrac56$ is the argument of
$\omega_3 = \tfrac56 + \tfrac{\sqrt{11}}{6}i$ and $60^\circ$ that of
$\omega_1 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$ (Figure~\ref{fig:units}). Those two do
not by themselves give all thirty: $\omega_1$ and $\omega_3$ and their inverses
generate only the eighteen with $j$ even, and the twelve with $j$ odd need the
half-angle $\varphi = \tfrac{\sqrt{33}}{6} + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{6}i = e^{i\theta_3/2}$
as well---the same $\varphi$ that generates the lattice in \cite{Voronov}, met
again in Section~\ref{sec:seven}. The group is dense in the plane, and the reason is not that it has rank $4$: it
contains $\tfrac13$ and $\tfrac{\sqrt{33}}3$, hence the subgroup
$\tfrac13\Z + \tfrac{\sqrt{33}}3\Z$, which is dense in the real axis because
$\sqrt{33}$ is irrational; and it is carried to itself by the rotation through
$60^\circ$, which supplies a second, independent line. A closed subgroup of
$(\C,+)$ containing two independent dense lines is all of $\C$. Rank $4$ alone would
not give it: $(\Z + \sqrt2\,\Z + \sqrt3\,\Z) \times \Z$ has rank $4$ and is not
dense. Nor does $\varphi \in \Lat$ make $\varphi\Lat = \Lat$---$\Lat$ is no
ring, and $\varphi^3 = (\sqrt{33} + 4i\sqrt3)/9$ is already outside it.

A warning about the name, and then the fact that replaces it. $\Lat$ is a
\emph{group}, not a ring: it is not closed under multiplication---$\omega_3^2 =
\tfrac{7}{18} + \tfrac{5\sqrt{11}}{18}i$ is not in it, and of the $900$ products of
pairs of unit vectors only $684$ are. But $3\Lat$ \emph{is} a ring, and more than
that: it is the maximal order of the field $V$ that $\Lat$ spans, so
\[
  \Lat = \tfrac13\,\Ok_V, \qquad V = \Q(\sqrt{33},\, i\sqrt3),
\]
which is Proposition~\ref{prop:order} below and is what Sections \ref{sec:arith}
and \ref{sec:seven} actually run on.
The \emph{Moser ring} that $\Lat$ generates is a strictly larger object, with
denominators $3^k$; Gibbs' homomorphism and Hubai's $22$ colourings
\cite{Hubai} live there, while everything below is about $\Lat$ itself---as is
D\'ucz's uniqueness theorem \cite{Ducz} in spirit, though not literally: his
lattice is the smaller one generated by $1, \omega_1, \omega_3, \omega_1\omega_3$,
of index $9$ in $\Lat$, a difference Section~\ref{sec:arith} returns to. What $\Lat$ \emph{is} closed under is
multiplication by $\Ok = \Z[(1+\sqrt{33})/2]$, and Section \ref{sec:arith} runs
on that alone.

Take $L, S \subseteq \Lat$, rotate the second by a fixed $\theta$, and glue them
at the origin:
\[
  G = L \cup \theta(S).
\]
Parts calls this \emph{type~M}, after Heule, and describes it as two base graphs
rotated relative to each other so as to obtain many additional unit edges of two
or more kinds. It is one of four types he classifies---A, M, J and T---and the
one at issue here: type~M, he writes, ``is of the greatest interest, because so
far it gives 5-chromatic graphs with the least number of vertices''. The only rotation he records
for it is $\theta_4 = \arccos(7/8)$, which he writes as
$\rho = \exp(i\arccos 7/8)$; writing in 2020 he could not have had the four that
\cite{Voronov} found the year after, and Section~\ref{sec:seven} restores them.
Of the family as a whole he adds \cite{Parts}:
\begin{quote}
``Working constructions of type M with other rotations are not known yet.''
\end{quote}
That sentence is the question this paper answers.

\subsection*{The rotations at issue}
Polymath16's algebraic formulation \cite{Polymath} indexes the natural rotations
by $\omega_t = \exp\!\big(i\arccos(1 - 1/(2t))\big)$, the apex angle of a
$\sqrt{t}, \sqrt{t}, 1$ isosceles triangle. The notation agrees with the one
above rather than colliding with it: $\omega_1$ and $\omega_3$ in this sense have
cosines $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac56$, so they are exactly the two generators of
$\Lat$ already named. We write $\theta_a$ for the same rotation at squared radius
$a$, allowing any rational $a > 1/4$---the square root is real for $a \ge 1/4$, and
$a = 1/4$ is the degenerate endpoint where $\theta_a = -1$ and the shell pairing
collapses, so we exclude it---and then $4 \mid a$ is to be read
$2$-adically, as $v_2(a) \ge 2$, which for integral $a$ is ordinary divisibility:
\[
  \cos\theta_a = \frac{2a-1}{2a},
  \qquad
  \sin\theta_a = \frac{\sqrt{4a-1}}{2a}.
\]

\subsection*{One symmetry, and the normalisation it forces}
$\Lat$ is carried to itself by complex conjugation and by $-1$, so for any
$L, S \subseteq \Lat$,
\[
  \overline{L \cup \theta_a(S)} \;=\; \overline L \cup
  \theta_{a'}\bigl(-\overline S\bigr), \qquad a' = \frac{a}{4a-1},
\]
with $\overline L$ and $-\overline S$ again inside $\Lat$. The identity behind it
is $\theta_{a'} = -\overline{\theta_a}$, which one reads off
$\cos\theta_{a'} = -\cos\theta_a$ and $\sin\theta_{a'} = \sin\theta_a$. Mirror
images are isometric, so $\theta_a$ joins if and only if $\theta_{a'}$ does. The
map $a \mapsto a/(4a-1)$ is an involution exchanging $(\tfrac14,\tfrac12)$ with
$(\tfrac12,\infty)$ and fixing $\tfrac12$, and it leaves $v_2(a)$ alone whenever
the denominator of $a$ is odd.

\emph{We normalise to $\cos\theta_a \ge 0$, that is to $a \ge \tfrac12$}, and every
statement below about which $\theta_a$ join is a statement about such
representatives. This is not cosmetic. It is the shell that breaks the symmetry:
$\theta_4$ spins a shell of $30$ points, while its partner $\theta_{4/15}$---which
carries the mirror image of the very same $509$-vertex graph---has an empty shell,
since $15 \notin \{1,3,9\}$. Un-normalised, a criterion phrased through shells
would admit one of the pair and reject the other.

Then $\lvert p - \theta_a(p)\rvert = \lvert p\rvert \lvert 1-\theta_a\rvert =
\lvert p \rvert/\sqrt a$, so a point and its own image are a unit pair exactly
when $\lvert p\rvert = \sqrt a$: the rotation's own pairing lives on that
\emph{shell} and nowhere else, and Figure~\ref{fig:spindle} is the whole
mechanism in one picture. Other points do acquire cross edges---to images of
\emph{other} points, as Figure~\ref{fig:orbits} shows---but if the shell is empty
the rotation has nothing of its own to act on.

Which $\theta_a$ are useful has been approached experimentally. Voronov,
Neopryatnaya and Dergachev \cite{Voronov} enumerate rotations carrying one subset
of vertices onto another, and their first series---built on the Moser spindle,
with the generators $\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$ and
$\tfrac{\sqrt{33}}{6}+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{6}i$, so on $\Lat$---reports five that work
(their Table 5), Heule's among them. So five working type-M rotations were known,
not one.

They are, however, one orbit. All five have the form $\rho u$ with $\rho$ Heule's
rotation and $u$ one of the $30$ unit vectors of $\Lat$: the arguments of the
five $u$ are $0$, $-\theta_3/2$, $-\theta_3$, $\theta_3/2 - 60^\circ$ and
$\theta_3 - 60^\circ$, the last two matching $\rho u$ only up to complex
conjugation. Multiplying by $u$ does \emph{not} fix $\Lat$---four of the
five move it, so the joint lattices genuinely differ---but of the five rotations
exactly one has rational cosine, namely $\rho = \theta_4$ with $\cos = 7/8$. A
spindle is precisely a rotation of rational cosine, so the other four are not
spindles: in the family this paper classifies, exactly one working member was
known. No criterion, and no classification, has been given for either family.

\subsection*{What is proved here}

In order of weight, and with the section that proves each.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{A divisibility obstruction that decides every rational $a$.} For a
  rank-$8$ joint lattice, $4 \nmid a$ gives a homomorphism $4$-colouring and so
  kills the rotation outright; $4 \mid a$ with an occupied shell admits none.
  Theorem~\ref{thm:law}, Section~\ref{sec:proof}. The rank hypothesis is itself
  arithmetic and not an assumption: Proposition~\ref{prop:rank}.
\item \textbf{$3\Lat$ is the maximal order of $\Q(\sqrt{33}, i\sqrt3)$}
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:order}), which is what the rest runs on. It identifies
  $\Lat$'s two integral geometric $4$-colourings as the reductions modulo the two
  primes above $2$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:primes}), and makes shell occupancy a
  relative-norm question.
\item \textbf{Shell occupancy in closed form}, for every rational radius:
  Theorem~\ref{prop:shells}, by Hasse's norm theorem. The rotations passing both
  it and the law have natural density zero (Corollary~\ref{cor:closed} and after).
\item \textbf{An infinite family of joins.} Chaining a monochromatic-pair gadget
  of Parts' gives a $5$-chromatic join at $a = 64n^2/9$ for every $n \ge 1$
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:chain}), including $a = 64$, the one integer spindle the filters
  of Section~\ref{sec:stronger} cannot reach.
\item \textbf{Seven joining spindles}, three of them without precedent we could
  find (Section~\ref{sec:seven}); and eleven further integer candidates killed by
  a periodic refinement (Section~\ref{sec:stronger}).
\item \textbf{Five cross edges are necessary} at $\theta_4$, and exactly $1920$
  five-edge sets defeat every pairing of homomorphism colourings
  (Theorems~\ref{thm:five}--\ref{thm:profile}). Whether any is realised by a
  5-chromatic graph is open.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The record of $509$ is untouched, and nothing here brings it closer.
What is \emph{not} settled is listed in Section~\ref{sec:notclaimed}.

\begin{theorem}[the divisibility law]\label{thm:law}
Let $a$ be a positive rational and suppose the joint lattice
$\Lam_a = \Lat + \theta_a(\Lat)$ has rank~$8$---which by
Proposition~\ref{prop:rank} is every $a$ except those with $4a-1 = 3k^2$ or
$11k^2$ for a rational $k$. If $4 \nmid a$ then $\Lam_a$ admits a homomorphism
$4$-colouring. If $4 \mid a$ and the shell of $\Lat$ at radius $\sqrt a$ is
non-empty, it admits none.
\end{theorem}

The shell hypothesis carries only the second half, and it is not decoration: at
$a = 8$, $32$, $40$ and $56$ the shell is empty and a homomorphism colouring does
exist, while at $a = 24$, empty as well, none does. An empty shell decides
nothing either way, which is why Section~\ref{sec:seven} asks for occupancy
separately.

A homomorphism $4$-colouring properly colours the \emph{entire} infinite lattice,
at every radius, so:

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:kill}
If $\Lam_a$ has rank $8$ and $4 \nmid a$, no subset of $\Lam_a$ is 5-chromatic;
in particular no 5-chromatic $L \cup \theta_a(S)$ exists, at any size.
\end{corollary}

Neither hypothesis is an obstacle to check case by case. The second is an
explicit and recognisable exclusion, and the first is decided outright by
Proposition~\ref{prop:rank}: the rank is $8$ unless $4a-1$ is $3k^2$ or $11k^2$
for rational $k$, and those excluded $a$ are not covered by anything here.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:rank}
Let $V \subset \C$ be the $\Q$-span of $\Lat$. Then $V = \Q(\sqrt{33},\,i\sqrt3)$,
a field of degree $4$ over $\Q$, and
\[
  \theta_a \in V
  \iff
  4a-1 = 3k^2 \text{ or } 11k^2 \text{ for a \emph{rational} } k.
\]
If $\theta_a \in V$ then $\Lam_a = \Lat + \theta_a(\Lat)$ has rank $4$; otherwise
it has rank exactly $8$ and Theorem~\ref{thm:law} applies. No intermediate rank
occurs.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The chart \eqref{eq:chart} with rational $A,B,C,D$ gives $V$ the $\Q$-basis
$1, \sqrt{33}, i\sqrt3, i\sqrt{11}$. Since $i\sqrt{11} = (i\sqrt3)\sqrt{33}/3$
and $(i\sqrt3)^2 = -3$, this is $\Q(\sqrt{33})(i\sqrt3)$, of degree $2$ over
$\Q(\sqrt{33})$ and $4$ over $\Q$; in particular $V$ is a \emph{field}.

Now $\theta_a = c + is$ with $c = (2a-1)/(2a) \in \Q$ and
$s = \sqrt{4a-1}/(2a)$, so $\theta_a \in V$ if and only if $is \in V$, that is
$\sqrt{4a-1} \in \Q\sqrt3 + \Q\sqrt{11}$. Writing
$\sqrt{4a-1} = p\sqrt3 + q\sqrt{11}$ and squaring gives
$4a-1 = 3p^2 + 11q^2 + 2pq\sqrt{33}$, so $pq = 0$ and $4a-1$ is $3p^2$ or
$11q^2$, with $p, q$ rational. When $a$ is an integer the rational $k$ is forced
to be one: if $4a-1 = 3(u/v)^2$ in lowest terms then $v^2 \mid 3$, so $v = 1$;
and then $k$ is odd besides, since $4a-1 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ while $3k^2$ and
$11k^2$ are $0$ mod $4$ for even $k$. For non-integer $a$ the rational form is
the one that bites: $a = 1/3, 7/3, 13/3, 31/3$ and $43/3$ all have
$4a-1 \in 3\Q^2$, all have rank $4$, and all have a non-empty shell, so they are
genuinely excluded from Theorem~\ref{thm:law} and not vacuously.

If $\theta_a \in V$ then multiplication by it is a bijection of the field $V$, so
$\theta_a V = V$ and $\Lam_a \subset V$ has rank $4$. If $\theta_a \notin V$,
suppose $w \ne 0$ lay in $V \cap \theta_a V$, say $w = \theta_a v$ with
$v \in V \setminus \{0\}$. Then $\theta_a = wv^{-1} \in V$, because $V$ is a
field---a contradiction. So $V \cap \theta_a V = 0$ and
$\dim_\Q(V + \theta_a V) = 4 + 4 = 8$.
\end{proof}

That $\Lat$ itself is not closed under multiplication---$\omega_3^2 =
\tfrac{7}{18} + \tfrac{5\sqrt{11}}{18}i$ is not in it---costs nothing here: the
argument needs only its $\Q$-span to be. The excluded integers up to $100$ are $1, 3, 7, 19, 25, 37, 61, 69$ and $91$,
and the smallest excluded rationals with admissible denominator are
$1/3, 7/3, 13/3, 31/3$ and $43/3$. A thin set either way, and not a surprising
one,
since $\theta_1$ is $\omega_1$ and $\theta_3$ is $\omega_3$: these are rotations
the lattice already contains. The direct rank computation agrees everywhere it has
been run, on the integers and on the rational cases above; the accompanying code
reports the range.

Two words in the statement are used throughout in the following senses. A
$4$-colouring of $\Lat$ is \emph{integral} if its colour classes are the cosets of
a subgroup of index $4$---equivalently, if it is a group homomorphism onto a group
of order four, which is Gibbs' construction and the only kind considered in this
paper. It is \emph{geometric} if whether a pair of points is monochromatic depends
on the distance between them and nothing else; a geometric colouring is therefore
carried to itself by every isometry of $\Lat$ fixing the origin, and in particular
by multiplication by $\omega = e^{i\pi/3}$. Both conditions are used below, and
used separately: integrality is what puts the question inside $\Lat/2\Lat$, and
geometry is what forces the quotient to be Klein rather than cyclic.

\begin{theorem}[the colourings are prime reductions]\label{thm:primes}
The rational prime $2$ splits in $\Q(\sqrt{33})$ as $-\pi_1\pi_2$ with
$\pi_{1,2} = (5 \pm \sqrt{33})/2$. The lattice $\Lat$ is a module over
$\Ok = \Z[(1+\sqrt{33})/2]$, and the two integral geometric $4$-colourings of
$\Lat$---there are exactly two, as the proof below counts---are the reductions
modulo $\pi_1\Lat$ and $\pi_2\Lat$. In particular there are two of them because $2$ splits into two
primes.
\end{theorem}

Theorem \ref{thm:primes} is what makes the proof of Theorem \ref{thm:law} short:
``this colour vanishes'' becomes ``this prime divides''. Section \ref{sec:seven}
exhibits six further joining spindles, so the answer to Parts' question is that at
least seven join in this family, four of them type M in his own sense. Two of the
six are not new in the way the others are, and it is worth saying so before
claiming them. Parts published two minimal monochromatic-pair gadgets, at
distances $8/3$ and $8/\sqrt3$; doubling one about a terminal gives a
$5$-chromatic join at the rotation that spins it, so $\alpha_{64/9}$ and
$\alpha_{64/3}$ already carry joins on $733$ and $841$ vertices. Chaining the
first gadget with itself gives $\alpha_{256/9}$ the same way, as
Section~\ref{sec:seven} sets out. All three of our rational spindles are
therefore consequences of gadgets he had in hand, and what is added at them is the
field they live in---which an earlier reading had wrongly ruled out---the
ball-union form, and the arithmetic that decides in advance which rotations can
work at all. For the three integer spindles $\theta_{16}$, $\theta_{28}$ and $\theta_{36}$ we
have found no prior construction, on the search Section~\ref{sec:seven} describes;
$\theta_4$ is the record and was known. Counting
rotations rather than constructions, at least eleven join in the broader two-copy
class: the five already known and these six. Eight of the eleven are type M in
Parts' sense, where he recorded one.

\subsection*{What is not new}
The idea of colouring the lattice by a group homomorphism onto a four-element
group is Philip Gibbs', from Polymath16 in 2018 \cite{Polymath}: he showed the
Moser ring admits one. Our criterion is that idea aimed at the \emph{joint}
lattice of a candidate rotation rather than at the Moser ring, and the
contribution here is the classification it yields, not the tool. The $\omega_t$
notation and the algebraic framework are Polymath16's.

That a lattice of this kind has exactly two integral geometric $4$-colourings is
D\'ucz's theorem \cite{Ducz}, his Theorem 3.2, and the two colourings themselves
are older still: D\'ucz credits Hubai \cite{Hubai}, who found them in the fourth
Polymath16 thread in 2018 among $22$ essentially different $4$-colourings of the
Moser ring. We claim neither. One caution about the attribution: D\'ucz states his
theorem for the lattice he defines, generated by $1, \omega_1, \omega_3$ and
$\omega_1\omega_3$, which sits inside $\Lat$ with index $9$---it misses $1/3$, and
twelve of $\Lat$'s thirty unit vectors---so his theorem and the count below are
about different groups, and we do not transfer either to the other. The count
below is proved here, for $\Lat$, and stands on its own. What Section \ref{sec:arith} adds is the
\emph{reason} for the number two---$33 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$, so $2$ splits into
exactly two primes---which is a structural explanation neither of them gives:
D\'ucz's proof is a sublattice argument on coordinates mod $2$ and contains no
prime ideal and no number field.

Two more near misses worth naming, because a reader will wonder. De Grey's 2018
construction \cite{deGrey} does use a second rotation, by $2\arcsin\tfrac18$,
which is our $\theta_{16}$; but it is applied about a point other than the
origin---his graph $L$ is $K$ together with a copy of $K$ rotated about a vertex
$A$---so it is not a type-M join of two lattices, and the final graph there turns
about $(-2,0)$ by $\pi/2 \pm \arcsin\tfrac18$. It is suggestive, not a second
data point. And Parts \cite{Parts} already writes his vertices over $\sqrt{33}$,
in his own normalisation---denominator $4\cdot 3^{h}$ where ours is $12$---as
$(A + B\sqrt{33} + iC\sqrt3 + iD\sqrt{11})/(4\cdot 3^{h})$ subject to
$A - B + C + D \in 4\Z$. (His coordinates are put in capitals here; lower-case
$a$ is the squared radius throughout this paper.) That congruence normalises coordinates inside one fixed
lattice; the divisibility here is a condition on the \emph{rotation}, deciding
before any graph is built whether a join can be 5-chromatic at all. The
ingredients were on the page; the test was not.

\section{The joint lattice, and why no ball is needed}\label{sec:ball}

Every earlier test of a rotation built a ball of lattice points, found its unit
edges and ran a SAT solver. That has a free parameter---the ball---and a negative
answer about a ball is not an answer about the lattice: a rotation whose shell at
radius $\sqrt a$ lies outside the ball is being dismissed on a graph that never
contained a single pair $p, \theta_a(p)$. Section~\ref{sec:seven} exhibits six
rotations that a ball at the record's own scale misses for exactly that reason.

No ball is needed. Write $W$ for the $30$ unit vectors of $\Lat$. For a candidate
$\theta$, both halves of $L \cup \theta(S)$ lie in
\[
  \Lam_\theta = \langle W \cup \theta(W) \rangle,
\]
the group they and their images generate: a finitely generated subgroup of
$(\C,+)$ with no radius in it. Its unit vectors are
enumerable exactly: $q(z) = \Tr(z\bar z)$ is a positive-definite quadratic form on
$\Lam_\theta \otimes \Q$, and a unit vector has $z\bar z = 1$ and hence
$q(z) = \Tr(1)$, so the unit vectors lie \emph{among} the integer points of one
ellipsoid. That set is finite and can be listed exactly; sifting it by the exact
condition $z\bar z = 1$---which is two conditions where $q$ is one---leaves the
unit vectors, a complete list obtained without reference to any ball.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{The criterion.} Look for a group homomorphism
$\varphi : \Lam_\theta \to \mathcal{A}$ with $\lvert \mathcal{A} \rvert = 4$ and $\varphi(u) \ne 0$
for every unit vector $u$. If one exists then for any $p,q \in \Lam_\theta$ at
distance $1$ the difference $p-q$ is a unit vector, so $\varphi(p) \ne \varphi(q)$
and $p \mapsto \varphi(p)$ is a proper colouring of the whole infinite lattice.
Hence
\[
  \varphi \text{ exists} \implies \text{no subset of } \Lam_\theta \text{ is 5-chromatic, ever.}
\]
The converse fails---a lattice may be $4$-colourable by a colouring that is not a
homomorphism---so the test is a \emph{one-sided kill}, and a survivor is a
candidate and nothing more. There are two groups of order $4$, the Klein group and
$\Z/4$, and both must be ruled out.

A one-sided kill has one way to be wrong: killing something that works. Every
rotation known to carry a 5-chromatic type-M graph is therefore a test of the
criterion, and there are five---Heule's and the four of \cite{Voronov}. Run
against all five, the criterion returns \emph{unknown} each time and kills none;
the five joint lattices have rank $8$ and carry $126$, $108$, $108$, $90$ and
$90$ unit vectors respectively. That is the only outcome consistent with their
being 5-chromatic, and it is not automatic.

\section{The criterion in closed form}\label{sec:closed}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:half}
Suppose $\Lam_\theta$ contains a vector of length $1/2$. Then no homomorphism
onto a group of order $4$---Klein or cyclic---avoids every unit vector.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $\lvert h \rvert = 1/2$, so $2h$ is a unit vector of $\Lam_\theta$. In the
Klein group every element satisfies $2x = 0$, so $\varphi(2h) = 2\varphi(h) = 0$
and the unit vector $2h$ is killed.

In $\Z/4$, $\varphi(2h) = 2\varphi(h) \ne 0$ forces $\varphi(h)$ odd, and that is
already impossible. Write $\omega = e^{i\pi/3}$, a unit vector of $\Lat$ with
$\omega\Lat = \Lat$, so $\omega\Lam_\theta = \Lam_\theta$ and $\omega h$,
$\omega^2 h$ are half-unit vectors alongside $h$. Since $\omega^2 = \omega - 1$,
\[
  h + \omega h + \omega^2 h \;=\; 2\omega h \;\in\; 2\Lam_\theta ,
\]
so $\varphi(h) + \varphi(\omega h) + \varphi(\omega^2 h) = 2\varphi(\omega h)$ is
even, while three odd values sum to an odd one.
\end{proof}

Both halves are unconditional, and the $\omega$-identity is why: it is a relation
of \emph{odd} weight among half-unit vectors, and it exists wherever one
half-unit vector does. Swept over every integer spindle to $a = 100$ that has
one---sixteen of them---the relation is found every time, as it must be.

\begin{remark}
An earlier version of this section reported the relation as \emph{failing} at
$a = 48$, $64$ and $80$, and called the cyclic half conditional on that account.
The report was an artefact. The sweep collected the images of the half-unit
vectors in $\Lam_\theta/2\Lam_\theta$ and discarded the zero class before testing;
at those three values \emph{every} half-unit vector lies in $2\Lam_\theta$---all
$42$, $30$ and $60$ of them---so discarding zero emptied the system, and the
empty system read as no relation. A half-unit vector in $2\Lam_\theta$ is in fact
the strongest case of all: it is a relation of weight one, since no homomorphism
sends $0$ to an odd residue.
\end{remark}

Half-unit vectors are therefore the object to count, and for a spindle they have a
closed form. For $p$ on the shell, $p$ and $\theta_a(p)$ are both at distance
$\sqrt a$, and
\[
  \tfrac12\big(p - \theta_a(p)\big)
\]
has length exactly $1/2$ for every $a$, since $\lvert 1 - \theta_a \rvert =
1/\sqrt a$. Now $p - \theta_a(p)$ always lies in $\Lam_a$, being a difference of
one element of $\Lat$ and one of $\theta_a(\Lat)$; whether \emph{half} of it does
is another matter, and that is the whole content of the classification:
$\tfrac12(p-\theta_a(p)) \in \Lam_a$ exactly when $p - \theta_a(p) \in 2\Lam_a$.

\section{The arithmetic of the Moser lattice}\label{sec:arith}

\subsection{A module over a real quadratic ring}
In the chart
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chart}
  z = \frac{A + B\sqrt{33}}{12} + i\,\frac{C\sqrt3 + D\sqrt{11}}{12},
  \qquad A,B,C,D \in \Z,
\end{equation}
the lattice $\Lat$ is cut out by the congruences
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cong}
  \text{(i)}\quad A \equiv B \equiv C \equiv D \pmod 2,
  \qquad
  \text{(ii)}\quad A + B + C + D \equiv 2A \pmod 4,
\end{equation}
sixteen of the $256$ residue classes mod $4$. (The chart without them is a group
containing $\Lat$ with index $16$, so reading a chart count as a lattice count
overcounts---at rational squared radii at least; Lemma~\ref{lem:auto} below says
it never does at integer ones.) Squared lengths are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:norm}
  \lvert z \rvert^2
  = \frac{\big(A^2 + 33B^2 + 3C^2 + 11D^2\big) + 2(AB + CD)\sqrt{33}}{144},
\end{equation}
so they lie in $\Q(\sqrt{33})$, and are \emph{rational exactly when
$AB + CD = 0$}, in which case they lie in $\tfrac1{144}\Z$. Write
\[
  S = A^2 + 33B^2 + 3C^2 + 11D^2
\]
for the numerator, so a rational squared length is $S/144$. (This $S$ is the
form's value throughout Sections \ref{sec:arith} and \ref{sec:proof}; the small
half of a construction carries the same letter only outside them.)

At \emph{integer} squared radii the congruences cost nothing, which is worth
recording because it is what makes the chart safe to compute with there.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:auto}
Let $A,B,C,D \in \Z$ satisfy $AB + CD = 0$ and $16 \mid S$. Then \eqref{eq:cong}
holds automatically.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Squares modulo $16$ depend only on their bases modulo $8$, so both hypotheses and
both conclusions are determined by $(A,B,C,D)$ modulo $8$, and $8^4 = 4096$
classes decide the statement. Of those, $320$ satisfy $16 \mid S$ while failing
\eqref{eq:cong}, and not one of the $320$ has $AB+CD \equiv 0 \pmod 8$.
\end{proof}

If $\lvert z\rvert^2 = a$ is a positive integer then $S = 144a$, so $16 \mid S$
and $AB + CD = 0$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:auto} applies: every integral chart point
at an integer squared radius already lies in $\Lat$. Measured, the two agree
exactly---identical counts at every $a \le 300$, not merely the same emptiness.

The module structure is in fact a great deal more than a module structure.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:order}
$3\Lat$ is the maximal order $\Ok_V$ of $V = \Q(\sqrt{33}, i\sqrt3)$; that is,
$\Lat = \tfrac13\Ok_V$. Writing $F = \Q(\sqrt{33})$ for the real quadratic
subfield and $\bar{\phantom{z}}$ for complex conjugation, which generates
$\Gal(V/F)$,
\[
  \lvert z \rvert^2 = z\bar z = \Nm_{V/F}(z),
\]
so a squared radius of $\Lat$ is one ninth of a relative norm of an algebraic
integer.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write coordinates over the $\Q$-basis $(1, \sqrt{33}, i\sqrt3, i\sqrt{11})$ of
$V$. Reducing the $30$ generators of $\Lat$ to Hermite form and multiplying by
$3$ gives a $\Z$-basis of $3\Lat$:
\[
  b_1 = \tfrac14(1,1,1,-1), \quad
  b_2 = \tfrac12(0,1,0,-7), \quad
  b_3 = \tfrac12(0,0,1,1), \quad
  b_4 = (0,0,0,1).
\]
It contains $1$, because $\tfrac13 \in \Lat$ by \eqref{eq:chart} at $(4,0,0,0)$,
and it is closed under multiplication: the sixteen products $b_ib_j$ reduce to
integer combinations of the $b_k$ by the rules $\sqrt3\sqrt{11} = \sqrt{33}$,
$\sqrt3\sqrt{33} = 3\sqrt{11}$ and $\sqrt{11}\sqrt{33} = 11\sqrt3$. So $3\Lat$ is
an order in $V$. (The same closure holds on all $900$ products of unit vectors,
where $\Lat$ itself fails: only $684$ of them return.)

For the discriminant, $\Tr_{V/\Q}(x) = 4x_1$ on these coordinates, since the four
embeddings negate $\sqrt{33}$ and $i\sqrt3$ independently and kill every other
basis vector. Hence
\[
  \bigl(\Tr(b_ib_j)\bigr) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
      5 & -22 &   4 &  11\\
    -22 &-506 &  77 & 154\\
      4 &  77 & -14 & -22\\
     11 & 154 & -22 & -44
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \det = 1089 .
\]
Now compare with the field. Put $F = \Q(\sqrt{33})$, so $V = F(i\sqrt3)$ and
$\operatorname{disc}(F) = 33$ because $33 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Two standard facts
close the argument without computing $\operatorname{disc}(V)$ separately. First,
for any order $\mathcal O$ of $V$,
\[
  \operatorname{disc}(\mathcal O) = [\Ok_V : \mathcal O]^2 \operatorname{disc}(V).
\]
Second, the conductor--discriminant formula for the relative extension $V/F$ of
degree $2$ gives
$\operatorname{disc}(V) = \Nm_{F/\Q}(\mathfrak d_{V/F})\operatorname{disc}(F)^2$,
so $\operatorname{disc}(V)$ is a positive multiple of $33^2 = 1089$. Taking
$\mathcal O = 3\Lat$ and substituting,
\[
  1089 \;=\; [\Ok_V : 3\Lat]^2 \cdot \Nm_{F/\Q}(\mathfrak d_{V/F}) \cdot 1089 ,
\]
which forces both factors to be $1$. So $3\Lat = \Ok_V$, and as a by-product
$\mathfrak d_{V/F} = (1)$: $V/F$ is unramified at every finite prime, which is
what Theorem~\ref{prop:shells} will use.

The norm identity is the definition of $\Nm_{V/F}$: complex conjugation fixes
$\sqrt{33}$ and negates $i\sqrt3$, so it generates $\Gal(V/F)$ and
$z\bar z = \Nm_{V/F}(z)$.
\end{proof}

Two consequences follow, and one of them is the whole of Section
\ref{sec:seven}'s criterion.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\Lat$ \emph{is a module over} $\Ok = \Z[(1+\sqrt{33})/2]$, the ring of
  integers of $\Q(\sqrt{33})$. This is a finite check and not a sample. The
  congruences \eqref{eq:cong} depend only on $(A,B,C,D)$ modulo $4$, and exactly
  $16$ of the $256$ classes satisfy them. Multiplication by $\sqrt{33}$ sends
  $(A,B,C,D)$ to $(33B,\,A,\,11D,\,3C)$---using $\sqrt3\sqrt{33} = 3\sqrt{11}$
  and $\sqrt{11}\sqrt{33} = 11\sqrt3$---and carries each of the $16$ classes to
  one of the $16$. For $(1+\sqrt{33})/2$ the same map, halved, is integral
  because $A \equiv B$ and $C \equiv D \pmod 2$ on those classes, and again
  preserves them. Since $\rank_\Z \Lat = 4$ and $\rank_\Z \Ok = 2$, the lattice
  $\Lat$ has rank $2$ over $\Ok$.
  \item $\Lat$ \emph{is not a lattice} in the geometric sense. It is dense, for
  the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:intro}; its short vectors are as short as one likes, and a
  depth-$4$ ball already contains one with
  $\lvert v \rvert^2 = (23 - 4\sqrt{33})/9 \approx 0.0024$. Any argument wanting a
  smallest vector must restrict to \emph{rational} norms, where
  $\tfrac1{144}\Z$ supplies one.
\end{itemize}

The smallest rational squared norms of $\Lat$, decided exactly from \eqref{eq:norm}
together with \eqref{eq:cong}, are
\[
  \tfrac19,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac49,\ \tfrac59,\ \tfrac79,\ 1,\ \dots
\]
so $1/9$ is the least, attained by six points, and it is the only one below
$1/4$. The value $1/36$ is not attained at all, a fact used in Section
\ref{sec:proof}.

\subsection{The two colourings are prime reductions}
Since $33 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$, the prime $2$ splits in $\Q(\sqrt{33})$:
\[
  2 = -\pi_1\pi_2,
  \qquad
  \pi_1 = \frac{5+\sqrt{33}}{2},
  \qquad
  \pi_2 = \frac{5-\sqrt{33}}{2},
  \qquad
  \Nm(\pi_i) = -2 .
\]
Because $\Lat$ is an $\Ok$-module, $\pi_i\Lat$ is a subgroup, and
$[\Lat : \pi_i \Lat] = \Nm(\pi_i)^2 = 4$, exactly the index a $4$-colouring needs,
where $[\Lat : 2\Lat] = 16$ is four times too big.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:primes}]
First, the quotient has no choice but to be Klein. By integrality the colour
classes are the cosets of a subgroup $H$ of index $4$. By geometry the isometry
$\omega = e^{i\pi/3}$ of $\Lat$ permutes those classes, and it fixes the origin,
so it fixes the class containing it: $\omega H = H$, and $\omega$ induces an
automorphism $T$ of $\Lat/H$. Since $\omega^2 = \omega - 1$,
\[
  T^2 - T + I = 0 .
\]
If $\Lat/H$ were cyclic of order $4$ then $T = \pm I$, the only automorphisms it
has, and the left side would be $I$ or $3I$---neither zero in $\operatorname{End}(\Z/4)$. So
$\Lat/H$ is the Klein group, $2\Lat \subseteq H$, and the search may be confined to
subgroups containing $2\Lat$.

That done, a homomorphism onto the Klein group killing no unit vector is the same
thing as a subgroup of index $4$ containing $2\Lat$ and meeting none of the $30$
unit vectors, so the whole question lives in $\Lat/2\Lat$---and
Proposition~\ref{prop:order} says exactly what that quotient is. Multiplication by
$3$ carries $\Lat$ isomorphically onto $\Ok_V$ and $\pi_i\Lat$ onto
$\pi_i\Ok_V$, so $\Lat/\pi_i\Lat = \Ok_V/\pi_i\Ok_V$; and $\pi_i$ is inert in
$V$, since the completion of $F$ at $\pi_i$ is $\Q_2$---the prime $2$ splits---and
$-3 \equiv 5 \pmod 8$ generates its unramified quadratic extension. Each quotient
is therefore the field $\F_4$, and as $\pi_1, \pi_2$ are coprime the Chinese
remainder theorem gives a ring isomorphism
\[
  \Lat/2\Lat \;\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\; \Lat/\pi_1\Lat \times \Lat/\pi_2\Lat
  \;=\; \F_4 \times \F_4 ,
\]
under which the two prime reductions are the coordinate lines:
$\pi_1\Lat/2\Lat = 0 \times \F_4$ and $\pi_2\Lat/2\Lat = \F_4 \times 0$.

Now the unit vectors. A unit vector $u$ has $\lvert u \rvert^2 = 1$, so
$v_{\pi_i}(u) = 0$ at both primes by Lemma \ref{lem:val}: its class is invertible
in each factor. There are $3 \cdot 3 = 9$ such classes, and all nine occur. The
thirty unit vectors are the $\omega^k\varphi^j$ with $0 \le k < 6$ and
$\lvert j \rvert \le 2$, for the same $\omega = e^{i\pi/3}$ and the half-angle
$\varphi = e^{i\theta_3/2}$ of Section~\ref{sec:intro}; modulo $2\Lat$,
$\omega^3 = -1 \equiv 1$ and $\varphi^{-1} \equiv \varphi^2$, so their classes are
the nine with $k, j \in \{0,1,2\}$, and those nine are distinct. So the $9$ is a ceiling
attained, not a coincidence of a count.

Two lines finish the theorem. The image $W$ of an index-$4$ subgroup containing
$2\Lat$ is an $\F_2$-subspace of $\F_4 \times \F_4$ of dimension $2$. If it meets
no unit class then every element of $W$ has a zero coordinate, so
\[
  W \;=\; \big(W \cap (\F_4 \times 0)\big) \;\cup\; \big(W \cap (0 \times \F_4)\big) ,
\]
a union of two subgroups---and a group is never the union of two proper subgroups.
Hence $W$ lies inside one coordinate line, and both lines have $\F_2$-dimension
$2$, so $W$ \emph{is} that line. Exactly two subgroups survive, and they are the
prime reductions:
\[
  \ker\gamma_1 = \pi_1\Lat, \qquad \ker\gamma_2 = \pi_2\Lat .
\]
The count---two---is the one D\'ucz reaches for his own lattice, by enumeration
rather than by his sublattice argument, and reached here for $\Lat$, which is not
his lattice.

Both are geometric, which closes the circle: by Lemma \ref{lem:val},
$v_{\pi_i}(\lvert x\rvert^2) = 2v_{\pi_i}(x)$ for $x \ne 0$, so $x \in \pi_i\Lat$
if and only if $v_{\pi_i}(\lvert x \rvert^2) \ge 2$---membership in either kernel
is read off the squared length alone, and monochromatic pairs are decided by
distance.
\end{proof}

In coordinates, for checking: take the Hermite basis of $\Lat$ in the chart
\eqref{eq:chart}, rows in the order $(A,B,C,D)$,
\[
  b_1 = (1,1,1,-1), \quad b_2 = (0,2,0,-14), \quad
  b_3 = (0,0,2,2), \quad b_4 = (0,0,0,4),
\]
and write a class of $\Lat/2\Lat \cong \F_2^4$ as the integer whose bit $i$ is the
coefficient of $b_{i+1}$. Then
\[
  \pi_1\Lat/2\Lat = \{0,5,9,12\}, \qquad \pi_2\Lat/2\Lat = \{0,3,4,7\},
\]
the nine unit classes are the nine integers in neither list, and a sweep over all
$35$ two-dimensional subspaces of $\F_2^4$---the enumeration the argument above
replaces---returns those two and no others. (The masks depend on the basis and the
bit order; both are fixed here so the numbers can be checked.)

Stability is the wrong invariant, and it is worth saying which one fails, because
matching the $\Ok$-stable subgroups against D\'ucz's two is the natural first move
and cannot work. Multiplication by $\sqrt{33}$ is the \emph{identity} on
$\Lat/2\Lat$, since $33 \equiv 1$, so it constrains nothing; multiplication by
$(1+\sqrt{33})/2$ leaves eleven of the thirty-five two-dimensional subspaces
stable---the two eigenspaces and the nine sums of a line from each---not two. It
is avoiding the unit vectors that cuts the field to two, and the ring structure
above that names them.

The theorem counts the Klein colourings, and Section~\ref{sec:interface} needs the
cyclic ones too, so count them here. A homomorphism $\Lat \to \Z/4$ missing every
unit vector is determined by its values on a basis; writing those as
$\ell + 2s$ over $\F_2$ makes the condition one affine system per $\ell$, a sweep
over $2^4$ rather than $4^4$, exactly as in Proposition~\ref{prop:half}'s proof.
The answer is small enough to print. In the Hermite basis $b_1,\dots,b_4$ fixed
below, writing a homomorphism as the tuple $(\varphi(b_1),\dots,\varphi(b_4))$,
exactly twelve of the $256$ tuples kill no unit vector:
\[
  (0,0,2,1),\quad (0,1,0,0),\quad (1,0,1,3),\quad
  (1,1,0,0),\quad (1,1,2,1),\quad (1,3,1,3)
\]
and their negatives. Up to negation---the only non-trivial automorphism of
$\Z/4$---there are therefore exactly \emph{six}. So $\Lat$ carries eight homomorphism $4$-colourings altogether, two
Klein and six cyclic, each counted up to the automorphisms of its own group, and
it is that $8$ that Section~\ref{sec:interface} pairs with itself.

The colour condition is therefore divisibility in $\Ok$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:colour}
  \gamma_i(x) = 0 \iff \pi_i \mid x ,
\end{equation}
proved above and confirmed directly, point by point and for both colourings, on a
ball of the lattice; the accompanying code reports the size. The number of
colourings is two \emph{because $2$ splits into exactly two primes}: the subgroups
that avoid every unit class are the two prime reductions, and there are two of
those because $33 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$. That is a reason, and D\'ucz's sublattice
proof does not give one. Note also
$\pi_1\Lat \cap \pi_2\Lat = \pi_1\pi_2\Lat = 2\Lat$: both colours vanish exactly
on $2\Lat$.

\section{Proof of the divisibility law}\label{sec:proof}

Assume $\Lam = \Lat + \theta_a(\Lat)$ has rank $8$, so
$\Lam = \Lat \oplus \theta_a(\Lat)$ and a homomorphism $\varphi : \Lam \to \mathcal{A}$ is a
pair $(\varphi_1, \varphi_2)$, one per summand, and each $\varphi_i$ must avoid
the $30$ unit vectors of its own summand.

Take $\mathcal{A}$ to be the Klein group first. Its exponent is $2$, so
$2\Lat \subseteq \ker\varphi_i$ automatically---no geometric hypothesis is needed
and none is available, since a homomorphism avoiding the unit vectors need not be
geometric: Section~\ref{sec:arith} exhibits six cyclic ones that are not. What is
available is the enumeration inside Theorem~\ref{thm:primes}: a subgroup of index
$4$ containing $2\Lat$ and meeting none of the nine unit classes is $\pi_1\Lat$ or
$\pi_2\Lat$. The image cannot be smaller than the whole Klein group either. Were
it of order $2$, then $1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 2\omega$ would give
$\varphi_i(1) + \varphi_i(\omega) + \varphi_i(\omega^2) = \varphi_i(2\omega) = 0$,
while each of the three terms is the one non-zero element of an order-$2$ group
and their sum is that element again. So each $\varphi_i$ is reduction modulo
$\pi_1\Lat$ or $\pi_2\Lat$, and with the six automorphisms of the Klein group that
is at most $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 6 = 24$ candidates, whatever $a$ is. (Both reductions
happen to be geometric, by Theorem~\ref{thm:primes}, but that is a conclusion
here and not a hypothesis.)

The cyclic target $\mathcal{A} = \Z/4$ is disposed of once and for all by
Proposition~\ref{prop:half}, which Step 5 invokes; the six cyclic colourings
counted in Section~\ref{sec:arith} are the ones that survive on $\Lat$ alone and
play no part here. The question is which of
them a \emph{cross} unit vector $u = r + \theta(s)$, with $r,s \in \Lat$ both
non-zero, destroys. Figure~\ref{fig:orbits} shows the two orbits these fall into.

\subsection*{Step 1: the two parts are parallel}
Writing $w = r\bar s$,
\[
  \lvert u \rvert^2
  = \lvert r \rvert^2 + \lvert s \rvert^2
  + \frac{(2a-1)\operatorname{Re} w + \sqrt{4a-1}\operatorname{Im} w}{a}.
\]
Separate the coefficients over $F = \Q(\sqrt{33})$. By \eqref{eq:norm} the
squared norms $\lvert r \rvert^2$ and $\lvert s \rvert^2$ lie in $F$, and so does
$\operatorname{Re} w$; the chart's imaginary part is $(C\sqrt3 + D\sqrt{11})/12$
and $\sqrt{11} = \sqrt{33}/\sqrt3$, so $\operatorname{Im} w$ lies in
$\sqrt3\,F$. Then $\lvert u \rvert^2 = 1$ reads
\[
  \alpha + \beta\sqrt{3(4a-1)} = 1, \qquad \alpha, \beta \in F,
\]
and rank $8$ is exactly the statement that $\sqrt{3(4a-1)} \notin F$---which by
Proposition~\ref{prop:rank} fails only at $4a-1 = 3k^2$ or $11k^2$. Note this is
the $\Q$-span and not the field: at $a = 1/2$ the root $\sqrt{4a-1} = 1$ lies in
$\Q(\sqrt3,\sqrt{11})$ and the rank is $8$ regardless. So $\beta = 0$, the
$\sqrt{4a-1}$ term vanishes on its
own: $\operatorname{Im} w = 0$, that is, $s = \lambda r$ for a real $\lambda$.

\subsection*{Step 2: $\lambda = -1$ gives the shell}
Then $u = (1-\theta)r$, and $\lvert 1 - \theta \rvert = 1/\sqrt a$ forces
$\lvert r \rvert^2 = a$: the point $r$ lies on the shell and $u$ is a shell
displacement. Since $s = -r$ and the colour group has exponent $2$,
$\gamma(s) = \gamma(r)$: the pair of colours is \emph{diagonal}, $(c,c)$, and
$\varphi(u) = \psi_1(c) + \psi_2(c)$, where $\psi_1,\psi_2$ are the two
automorphisms---not the two primes, which keep the letter $\pi$ throughout. The
colour group has exponent $2$, so $\varphi(u) = 0$ exactly when
$\psi_1(c) = \psi_2(c)$. At $c = 0$ that holds for \emph{every} candidate, and
$c = 0$ is exactly $r \in 2\Lat$, both primes at once. For $c \ne 0$ it still
holds for those candidates whose two automorphisms happen to agree at $c$, which
is why the conclusion below asks for a relative $3$-cycle and not merely for
$c \ne 0$.

\subsection*{Step 3: $r \in 2\Lat \iff 4 \mid a$}
Here $S = 144a$ is an integer even when $a$ is not, because $A,B,C,D$ are.
Then $v_2(S) = 4 + v_2(a)$, so $4 \mid a$---that is
$v_2(a) \ge 2$---is exactly $64 \mid S$. Recall too that $\lvert r \rvert^2$ is
rational exactly when $AB + CD = 0$. Three lemmas settle it.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:A}
For $(A,B,C,D)$ satisfying \eqref{eq:cong} with $AB + CD = 0$ we have
$16 \mid S$. Equivalently, every rational squared norm of $\Lat$ lies in
$\tfrac19\Z$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For odd $x$ write $x^2 = 1 + 8k_x$; then $x \mapsto k_x \bmod 2$ is the
homomorphism $\varepsilon : (\Z/8)^\times \to \Z/2$ with $\varepsilon = 0$ on
$\pm1$ and $1$ on $\pm3$. If all four are odd then
$S \equiv 8\big(\varepsilon(A)+\varepsilon(B)+\varepsilon(C)+\varepsilon(D)\big)
\pmod{16}$, and $AB = -CD$ gives
\[
  \varepsilon(A)+\varepsilon(B) = \varepsilon(AB) = \varepsilon(-CD)
  = \varepsilon(C)+\varepsilon(D),
\]
so the bracket is even. If all four are even then $S = 4S'$ with
$S' = A'^2 + 33B'^2 + 3C'^2 + 11D'^2$, and mod $4$,
$S' \equiv [A'] + [B'] + 3[C'] + 3[D']$ with $[\,\cdot\,]$ the odd indicator; the
congruences make the number of odd entries $0$, $2$ or $4$, and of the six
two-element patterns $\{A',B'\}$ and $\{C',D'\}$ are excluded by
$A'B' + C'D' = 0$ (one product odd, the other even), while the rest give
$1 + 3 \equiv 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:B}
If $A,B,C,D$ are all odd and $AB + CD = 0$ then $S \equiv 16 \pmod{32}$. The
second hypothesis is not decorative: $(1,1,1,3)$ gives $S = 136 \equiv 8$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Refine $\varepsilon$ one step: for odd $x$ write $x^2 = 1 + 8k_x$ as before and
set $\eta(x) = k_x \bmod 4$, so $x^2 \equiv 1 + 8\eta(x) \pmod{32}$. From
$AB = -CD$ we get $A^2B^2 = C^2D^2$, whose two sides are $1 + 8(\eta_A+\eta_B)$
and $1 + 8(\eta_C+\eta_D)$ modulo $32$, the cross terms $64\eta\eta$ vanishing;
so $\eta_A + \eta_B \equiv \eta_C + \eta_D \pmod 4$. Expanding $S$ modulo $32$
gives $16 + 8(\eta_A + \eta_B - \eta_C - \eta_D) \equiv 16$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:C}
If $4 \mid X^2 + 33Y^2 + 3Z^2 + 11W^2$ then $X+Y+Z+W$ is even.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Modulo $4$ a square is the odd indicator, so the form is congruent to
$[X] + [Y] + 3[Z] + 3[W]$. One odd entry gives $1$ or $3$; three give
$5 \equiv 1$ or $7 \equiv 3$. Neither is $0$, so the number of odd entries is
even.
\end{proof}

\emph{Forward.} If $r = 2w$ with $w \in \Lat$ then $a = 4\lvert w \rvert^2$, and
Lemma \ref{lem:A} gives $\lvert w \rvert^2 = T/9$ for an integer $T$, so
$a = 4T/9$ and $v_2(a) = 2 + v_2(T) \ge 2$: that is $4 \mid a$, with no
integrality of $a$ used. (When $a$ is an integer the same line reads
$9 \mid T$.)

\emph{Backward.} If $4 \mid a$ then $64 \mid S$, so $32 \mid S$, which Lemma
\ref{lem:B} forbids for all-odd coordinates; by \eqref{eq:cong} they are all even,
and with $A = 2A'$ and so on, $S = 4S'$ with $16 \mid S'$ and $A'B' + C'D' = 0$.
The halves satisfy (i): Lemma \ref{lem:C} gives an even number of odd entries
among them, and two is impossible. Suppose exactly two of $A',B',C',D'$ are odd.
The pairs $\{A',B'\}$ and $\{C',D'\}$ are out at once: either would make one side
of $A'B' = -C'D'$ odd and the other even. That leaves the four \emph{mixed}
pairs, one entry from $\{A',B'\}$ and one from $\{C',D'\}$, and they all behave
the same way, for a reason visible in the coefficients of
$S' = A'^2 + 33B'^2 + 3C'^2 + 11D'^2$:
\[
  1 + 3 = 4, \qquad 1 + 11 = 12, \qquad 33 + 3 = 36, \qquad 33 + 11 = 44,
\]
every one of them $\equiv 4 \pmod 8$. An odd square is $\equiv 1 \pmod 8$, so the
two odd terms of $S'$ contribute $4$ modulo $8$. For the two even ones,
$A'B' = -C'D'$ forces them to have the same $2$-adic valuation $k \ge 1$---the odd
entry on each side contributes nothing---so together they contribute
$2^{2k}(\alpha b^2 + \beta d^2)$ with $b,d$ odd and $\{\alpha,\beta\}$ the
complementary pair of coefficients, which is again $\equiv 4 \pmod 8$. At $k = 1$
that is $4 \cdot (8j+4) \equiv 0 \pmod{16}$, and at $k \ge 2$ the factor
$2^{2k}$ already kills it. Either way $S' \equiv 4 \pmod 8$, so $S'$ is $4$ or
$12$ modulo $16$ and never $0$, against $16 \mid S'$.
They satisfy (ii): when all odd, reducing $A'B' = -C'D'$ mod $4$ with every entry
in $\{1,3\}$ leaves an odd number congruent to $3$, which is exactly (ii); when
all even it reduces to $A''+B''+C''+D''$ being even, and $16 \mid S'$ gives
$4 \mid S''$, so Lemma \ref{lem:C} supplies it. Hence $r \in 2\Lat$.
\hfill\qedsymbol

\subsection*{Step 4: every other $\lambda$ imposes nothing new}
Both $r$ and $s$ lie in $V = \Q(\sqrt{33}, i\sqrt3)$, which
Proposition~\ref{prop:rank} says is a field, so $\lambda = s/r$ lies in $V$ too;
being real it lies in $V \cap \R = \Q(\sqrt{33})$, the only real quadratic
subfield. Write $\lambda = \alpha/\beta$ with $\alpha,\beta$ coprime in $\Ok$---which
is possible because $\Ok$ is a principal ideal domain, $\Q(\sqrt{33})$ having class
number one---and put $v = r/\beta$. It lies in $\Lat$: B\'ezout gives
$x\alpha + y\beta = 1$, and then $v = xs + yr \in \Lat$ since $\Lat$ is an
$\Ok$-module, with $\beta v = r$ and $\alpha v = s$. Substituting $r = \beta v$, $s = \alpha v$
into $\lvert u \rvert^2 = 1$ and clearing denominators,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:star}
  \lvert v \rvert^2 \big[\, a(\alpha-\beta)^2 + (4a-1)\alpha\beta \,\big] = a .
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:val}
For non-zero $v \in \Lat$ and $\pi$ a prime of $\Ok$ above $2$,
$v_\pi(\lvert v \rvert^2) = 2\,v_\pi(v)$, where $v_\pi(v)$ is the largest $k$ with
$v \in \pi^k\Lat$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First, $9\lvert x \rvert^2 \in \Ok$ for every $x \in \Lat$---Lemma \ref{lem:A} is
its rational case---because $144\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert^2$ is a quadratic form with
values in $\Ok$, and both its values and its polarisations on the $30$ unit
vectors are divisible by $16$ there: $30$ checks and $\binom{30}{2} = 435$. So
$v_\pi\big(144\lvert x\rvert^2\big) \ge 4 = v_\pi(144)$ throughout $\Lat$.

Now write $v = \pi^k v_1$ with $\pi \nmid v_1$. Since $\pi$ is real,
$\lvert v \rvert^2 = \pi^{2k}\lvert v_1 \rvert^2$, so it is enough that
$\lvert v_1 \rvert^2$ be a $\pi$-unit, that is that
$v_\pi\big(144\lvert v_1\rvert^2\big) = 4$ exactly. Replacing $v_1$ by
$v_1 + \pi w$ changes $144\lvert v_1 \rvert^2$ by
$144\pi(v_1\bar w + \bar v_1 w) + 144\pi^2\lvert w \rvert^2$, of valuation at least
$5$ by the previous paragraph, so a valuation of $4$ depends only on the class of
$v_1$ in $\Lat/\pi\Lat \cong \F_2^2$. Its three non-zero classes are represented by the six
points of least norm, $\lvert v \rvert^2 = 1/9$---namely $\pm\tfrac13$ and
$\pm\tfrac16 \pm \tfrac{i\sqrt3}{6}$---and there
$144\lvert v\rvert^2 = 16 = (\pi_1\pi_2)^4$, of valuation exactly $4$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:E}
Let $\pi$ be a prime of $\Ok$ above $2$, and let the denominator of $a$ be
odd---which holds automatically in the only case Corollary~\ref{cor:even} uses,
namely $a$ even, since then the denominator of $a$ in lowest terms is odd; more
generally Theorem~\ref{prop:shells} forces it whenever the shell is non-empty,
since it puts the denominator in $\{1,3,9\}$. If $\pi$ divides none of $v$,
$\alpha$, $\beta$, then $a$ is odd.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The residue field $\Ok/\pi$ is $\F_2$, so $\alpha \equiv \beta \equiv 1$ there,
hence $\alpha - \beta \equiv 0$ and $\alpha\beta \equiv 1$, and the bracket in
\eqref{eq:star} is $\equiv 4a-1 \equiv 1 \pmod \pi$: writing $a = n/b$ with $b$
odd makes $4n - b$ odd, so $v_\pi(4a-1) = 0$. (With $4 \mid b$ that fails, which
is why the hypothesis is stated.) So
$\pi$ does not divide it, and \eqref{eq:star} gives
$v_\pi(\lvert v \rvert^2) = v_\pi(a) = v_2(a)$. With $\pi \nmid v$, Lemma
\ref{lem:val} makes the left side $0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:even}
If $4 \nmid a$ then neither prime divides $v$. If moreover $a$ is even then
$\pi_i$ divides exactly one of $r = \beta v$ and $s = \alpha v$, so by
\eqref{eq:colour} exactly one of $\gamma_i(r), \gamma_i(s)$ vanishes and
$\varphi(u) = 0 + c$ with $c \ne 0$: the vector imposes no condition on a
candidate colouring both summands the same way---which is all Step 6 asks
for---for every $\lambda$, rational or not.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write $a = n/b$ in lowest terms and clear $b$ in \eqref{eq:star}:
\[
  \lvert v \rvert^2 \big[\, n(\alpha-\beta)^2 + (4n-b)\alpha\beta \,\big] = n ,
\]
a bracket that lies in $\Ok$ whatever the parities, where the bracket of
\eqref{eq:star} need not. By Lemma \ref{lem:val} the factor $\lvert v\rvert^2$ has
valuation $2v_\pi(v)$, so the left side has valuation $2v_\pi(v) + v_\pi(\text{bracket})$;
the bracket lies in $\Ok$, so that second term is at least $0$, and comparing with
the right gives $2v_\pi(v) \le v_2(n)$---an inequality, which is all the proof
uses. If $b$ is odd then
$v_2(n) = v_2(a) \le 1$ because $4 \nmid a$; if $b$ is even then $n$ is odd and
$v_2(n) = 0$. Either way $v_\pi(v) = 0$.

Now let $a$ be even. Lemma \ref{lem:E} puts $\pi$ into one of $v, \alpha, \beta$;
the first is excluded, and $\alpha,\beta$ are coprime, so exactly one of them is
divisible---say $\pi \mid \beta$. Then $\pi \mid r$, while $\alpha \equiv 1$ and
$v \not\equiv 0$ mod $\pi$ leave $\gamma_i(s) = \gamma_i(v) \ne 0$.
\end{proof}

The first half of the corollary settles the whole of Steps 4 and 5 by itself, for
every $\lambda$ at once, and it is worth saying so before the case analysis that
found it. Fix a prime $\pi_i$. Since $\Ok/\pi_i = \F_2$, an $\alpha$ the prime
misses satisfies $\alpha \equiv 1$, so $\alpha v \equiv v$ modulo $\pi_i\Lat$;
hence $\gamma_i(s) = \gamma_i(\alpha v)$ is $0$ if $\pi_i \mid \alpha$ and
$\gamma_i(v)$ if not, and likewise $\gamma_i(r) = \gamma_i(\beta v)$. As
$\alpha,\beta$ are coprime the prime misses at least one of them, leaving three
cases and only three, for $4 \nmid a$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\pi_i \mid \beta$. Then $\gamma_i(r) = 0$ while
      $\gamma_i(s) = \gamma_i(v) \ne 0$, so $\varphi(u) \ne 0$ for every
      candidate: the vector imposes nothing at all.
\item $\pi_i \mid \alpha$. The same with $r$ and $s$ exchanged.
\item Neither. Then $\gamma_i(r) = \gamma_i(s) = \gamma_i(v)$ and the pair is the
      diagonal $(c,c)$ of Step 2, with $c \ne 0$.
\end{itemize}
So a cross vector with $\lambda \ne -1$ imposes nothing the shell displacements do
not, whatever $\lambda$ is---rational or not, $a$ odd or even. That disposes of
every $\lambda$, at odd and even $a$ alike, and the proof can go straight to the
conclusion. Appendix \ref{app:odd} records the finer analysis the trichotomy
replaced, and the sweep that checks both against every rank-$8$ spindle to
$a = 80$.

\subsection*{Step 5: conclusion}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:D}
For a shell point $r$, either both primes divide $r$---which happens exactly when
$4 \mid a$---or neither does.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Both divide if and only if $r \in \pi_1\Lat \cap \pi_2\Lat = 2\Lat$, which by
Step 3 is $4 \mid a$. Suppose exactly one does, say $r = \pi_1 w$. Then
$\lvert w \rvert^2 = a/\pi_1^2 = a(29 - 5\sqrt{33})/8$, so in the chart $w$ has
\[
  S = 522a, \qquad 2(AB+CD) = -90a .
\]
A non-empty shell puts $a$ in $\tfrac19\Z$ by Lemma \ref{lem:A}, and $9$ divides
both $522$ and $90$, so these are integers; it also makes $v_2(a) \ge 0$, so the
two cases below exhaust $4 \nmid a$. If $a$ is odd then $S \equiv 2 \pmod 4$, and
$S \equiv [A]+[B]+3[C]+3[D]$ takes the value $2$ only on the patterns
$\mathrm{ooee}$ and $\mathrm{eeoo}$, both of which violate (i). If
$v_2(a) = 1$, write $a = 2a'$ with $a'$ odd; then $S \equiv 4 \pmod 8$,
which all-odd coordinates cannot give since they force $S \equiv 0 \pmod 8$, so all
coordinates are even and $AB + CD = 4(A'B' + C'D')$, while $AB + CD = -45a = -90a'$
is $2 \pmod 4$.
\end{proof}

If $4 \mid a$ \emph{and the shell is non-empty}---this is the only place the
second hypothesis is used, and the only half of the theorem that needs it---Step 3
gives a shell point $r = 2w$, and then $(1-\theta_a)w$ lies in $\Lam_a$ with length
$\tfrac12$, so Proposition~\ref{prop:half} rules out both groups of order $4$ at
once. Concretely, the shell displacement $u = (1-\theta_a)r$ has both colours zero,
so $\varphi(u) = 0$ for all $24$ Klein candidates and none survives. If $4 \nmid a$, Lemma
\ref{lem:D} says no shell point is divisible by either prime, so both colourings
give it a non-zero colour: the shell displacements impose only diagonal pairs
$(c,c)$ with $c \ne 0$, uniformly over the shell and for either choice of
colouring. Fix one of the two and use it on both summands, which is what makes
the pairs diagonal at all. By Step 4 nothing else constrains, and two automorphisms
differing at every non-zero point satisfy $\psi_1(c) \ne \psi_2(c)$---the relative
permutation must be a $3$-cycle, and two of the six are---so at least four of the
twenty-four candidates survive, and a colouring exists. This proves Theorem \ref{thm:law}.
\hfill\qedsymbol

\section{Seven joining spindles, three of them new}\label{sec:seven}

The law leaves $4 \mid a$ with a non-empty shell as the candidates. Which $a$ have
one is decided by the form \eqref{eq:norm} and not by a ball, and has a closed
answer, because $\Lat$ is $\tfrac13\Ok_V$ and occupancy is a norm question.

Call a prime $p \ne 3, 11$ \emph{obstructed} when it splits in $\Q(\sqrt{33})$ and
stays inert in $\Q(\sqrt{-3})$. For odd $p$ that is the pair of symbols
\[
  \Big(\tfrac{33}{p}\Big) = 1
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \Big(\tfrac{-3}{p}\Big) = -1,
\]
and neither need be computed: since $33 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, quadratic
reciprocity gives $\big(\tfrac{33}{p}\big) = \big(\tfrac{p}{33}\big)$, and
$\big(\tfrac{-3}{p}\big) = 1$ exactly when $p \equiv 1 \pmod 3$. Both are
therefore fixed by $p$ modulo $33$, and running the $20$ classes coprime to $33$
leaves five:
\[
  p \text{ is obstructed}
  \iff
  p \equiv 2,\ 8,\ 17,\ 29 \text{ or } 32 \pmod{33}.
\]
The prime $2$ is obstructed too, straight from the definition---$33 \equiv 1
\pmod 8$ splits it, $-3 \equiv 5 \pmod 8$ keeps it inert---and it lands in the
class $2 \pmod{33}$ with the others, so the residue form covers it as well and
nothing below makes it a separate case. Below $200$ the obstructed primes are
$2, 17, 29, 41, 83, 101, 107, 131, 149, 167, 173$ and $197$; the residue form is
checked against the symbols for every odd prime below $20000$.

\begin{theorem}\label{prop:shells}
Let $a = n/b$ be a positive rational in lowest terms. Then $\Lat$ has a point at
squared radius $a$ if and only if $b \in \{1,3,9\}$ and $v_p(n)$ is even at every
obstructed $p$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:order}, $a$ is a squared radius of $\Lat$ if and only if
$9a = \Nm_{V/F}(w)$ for some $w \in \Ok_V$. Write $\varepsilon = 23 - 4\sqrt{33}$, a generator of the totally positive units
of $F$---the unit group is $\langle -1\rangle \times \langle\varepsilon\rangle$ and
$-1$ is negative at both real places, so those units are exactly the powers of
$\varepsilon$. Three further facts are used, and it is worth saying where each
comes from.

$V/F$ is cyclic of degree $2$ and unramified at every finite prime: that is the
by-product of Proposition~\ref{prop:order}'s discriminant comparison, not a new
assumption.

$h(F) = 1$, in two lines. The Minkowski bound for $F = \Q(\sqrt{33})$ is
$\tfrac12\sqrt{33} < 2.88$, so every ideal class contains an integral ideal of
norm at most $2$, that is the unit ideal or a prime above $2$. Both of those are
principal, since $2 = -\pi_1\pi_2$ with $\pi_{1,2} = (5 \pm \sqrt{33})/2$ in
$\Ok_F$. So the class group is trivial.

$\varepsilon = 23 - 4\sqrt{33}$ is the fundamental unit of $F$, and it is totally
positive: $23^2 - 33\cdot4^2 = 1$, and both conjugates $23 \mp 4\sqrt{33}$ are
positive. Since $-1$ is negative at both real places, the totally positive units
are exactly the powers of $\varepsilon$.

$h(V) = 1$, by Minkowski again, and the computation is small enough to give in
full. With $n = 4$, $r_2 = 2$ and $\lvert\operatorname{disc}(V)\rvert = 1089$ the
bound is
\[
  \Bigl(\frac4\pi\Bigr)^{2}\frac{4!}{4^4}\sqrt{1089}
  = \frac{16}{\pi^2}\cdot\frac{3}{32}\cdot 33 < 5.02 ,
\]
so every ideal class contains an integral ideal of norm at most $5$. Which primes
of $\Ok_V$ have norm that small? Above $2$: each $\pi_i$ is inert in $V$, so the
prime above it has norm $\Nm(\pi_i)^2 = 4$, and there is no prime of norm $2$.
Above $3$: the prime $\mathfrak p_3$ of $F$ ramifies, and splits in $V$ into two
primes of norm $3$. Above $5$: $33 \equiv 3 \pmod 5$ is a non-residue, so $5$ is
inert in $F$ and every prime above it has norm at least $25$. Hence the only
integral ideals of norm at most $5$ are the unit ideal, the two primes of norm $3$
and the two of norm $4$, and it is enough to generate those four.

The ones above $2$ are $\pi_1\Ok_V$ and $\pi_2\Ok_V$, principal already in
$\Ok_F$. For those above $3$, take
\[
  w = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + i(2\sqrt3 + \sqrt{11})\bigr) \in \Ok_V,
\]
which lies in $3\Lat$ because $w/3$ is the chart point $(2,0,4,2)$ of
\eqref{eq:chart}---the point $(6,0,12,6)$ verifies only $w \in \Lat$---and has
\[
  \Nm_{V/F}(w) = \lvert w \rvert^2
  = \tfrac14\bigl(1 + (2\sqrt3+\sqrt{11})^2\bigr)
  = \tfrac14\bigl(24 + 4\sqrt{33}\bigr) = 6 + \sqrt{33},
\]
so $\Nm_{V/\Q}(w) = 36 - 33 = 3$ and $(w)$ is one of the two; its conjugate under
$\sqrt{33} \mapsto -\sqrt{33}$ is the other. So the class group is trivial.

(\textsc{Pari} agrees, and \texttt{scripts/moser\_is\_ov.py} runs the check, but
the argument above does not need it.)

Which primes of $F$ are inert in $V$? Exactly those above an obstructed $p$,
together with the one above $11$. That is the content of the two symbols: $p$
splits in $F$ and stays inert in $\Q(\sqrt{-3})$ precisely when the prime above it
does not split in $V$.

\emph{Necessity.} Let $9a = \Nm(w)$ with $w \in \Ok_V$. Then $9a$ lies in
$\Ok_F \cap \Q = \Z$, so $b \mid 9$. At a prime $P$ of $F$ inert in $V$,
$v_P(\Nm(w)) = 2v_{P'}(w)$ is even. For $P$ above an obstructed $p$ we have
$v_P = v_p$ on rationals, so $v_p(9a)$ is even; as no obstructed $p$ equals $3$,
$v_p(a)$ is even too. (Above $11$ the prime ramifies in $F$, so $v_P = 2v_{11}$ is
even for every rational and the condition is empty.)

\emph{Sufficiency.} Suppose $b \mid 9$ and $v_p(a)$ is even at every obstructed
$p$. Then $9a$ is a positive rational, hence totally positive in $F$, and has even
valuation at every prime of $F$ inert in $V$. At the two infinite places $V$ is
totally imaginary, so the local norms there are the positive reals, which $9a$
meets; at a finite prime that splits there is no condition, and at an inert one the
condition is even valuation. By Hasse's norm theorem for the cyclic extension
$V/F$, $9a = \Nm(x)$ for some $x \in V^\times$.

To get an algebraic integer, build the ideal instead. Even valuation at the inert
primes is exactly what lets $(9a)\Ok_F$ be written as $\Nm_{V/F}(A)$ for an ideal
$A$ of $\Ok_V$; since $h(V) = 1$, $A = (w)$, and $\Nm(w)$ generates $(9a)$, so
$\Nm(w) = 9a\,u$ for a unit $u$ of $\Ok_F$. Both $9a$ and $\Nm(w) = \lvert w\rvert^2$
are totally positive, so $u$ is. The totally positive units of $F$ are the powers
of $\varepsilon$---the unit group is $\langle -1\rangle \times \langle
\varepsilon\rangle$ and $-1$ is negative at both places---and $\varepsilon = \Nm_{V/F}(\eta)$ for
\[
  \eta = i\bigl(2\sqrt3 - \sqrt{11}\bigr) \in \Ok_V,
\]
which is purely imaginary, so $\bar\eta = -\eta$ and
\[
  \Nm_{V/F}(\eta) = \eta\bar\eta = \bigl(2\sqrt3 - \sqrt{11}\bigr)^2
  = 12 + 11 - 4\sqrt{33} = 23 - 4\sqrt{33} = \varepsilon,
\]
while $\Nm_{V/\Q}(\eta) = 23^2 - 16\cdot 33 = 1$, so $\eta$ is a unit. It lies in
$\Ok_V = 3\Lat$ because $\eta/3 = i(2\sqrt3 - \sqrt{11})/3$ is the chart point
$(0,0,8,-4)$. Replacing $w$
by $w\eta^{-k}$ with $u = \varepsilon^{k}$ gives $\Nm(w) = 9a$ exactly.
\end{proof}

The ingredients are all small and all checkable: $h(F) = h(V) = 1$,
$\operatorname{disc}(V) = 1089$, the signature $(0,2)$, and
$\Nm_{V/F}(\eta) = \varepsilon$, which the accompanying code verifies along with
$3\Lat$ being the maximal order.
Where the parity comes from is set out below, after the density.

The denominator contributes nothing beyond being admissible, which is consistent:
$3$ is not obstructed, and the reason is local rather than a matter of
ramification---$-11$ is a square in $\Q_3$, so $\sqrt{-3}$ already lies in
$\Q_3(\sqrt{33})$ and the extension splits there, leaving no condition. At $11$ the
extension is inert, but $11$ ramifies in $\Q(\sqrt{33})$, so a rational has even
valuation at the prime above it and the condition is empty again. The restriction $b \in \{1,3,9\}$ is Lemma~\ref{lem:A}'s corollary from
Section~\ref{sec:proof}, and with it the criterion covers \emph{every} rational
radius, not merely the integers. The three rational spindles found below are all
$n = 64$ or $256$, powers of two with even
exponent, and all three have non-empty shells.

Put beside Theorem~\ref{thm:law} this describes the occupied-shell candidates off
the factorisation of $a$, with no ball and no search. It does not say which
candidates admit a 5-chromatic join: Section~\ref{sec:stronger}
kills eleven of the sixteen below $100$; $a = 64$ is the one it cannot reach,
and Lemma~\ref{lem:chain} settles that one by construction instead.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:closed}
Let $a \ge 1/2$, normalised as in the introduction, and write $a = n/b$ in
lowest terms. Then $\theta_a$ is a candidate---the law admits it and its shell is
non-empty---if and only if
\[
  b \in \{1,3,9\},
  \qquad
  v_p(n) \text{ is even at every obstructed } p,
  \qquad
  v_2(n) \ge 2 .
\]
\end{corollary}

Only the last clause is new: $2$ being obstructed, the middle clause already asks
for $v_2$ even, and $4 \mid a$ adds $v_2 \ge 2$, so between them
$v_2 \in \{2,4,6,\dots\}$.
That single line accounts for the nine empty multiples of $4$ below $100$ without
any computation: eight of them---$8, 24, 32, 40, 56, 72, 88, 96$---have $v_2$ odd,
and the ninth is $68 = 2^2\cdot 17$, where the obstructed prime $17$ appears once.
Checked against the form for every integer $a \le 400$, with no exception.

It also settles how common candidates are, and the answer is contrary to
appearances. Write $\mathcal C$ for the integer candidates. Each condition is a
restriction at one prime and the conditions at distinct primes are independent, so
for \emph{any} finite set $P$ of obstructed primes containing $2$,
\[
  \overline d(\mathcal C) \;\le\;
  \frac16 \prod_{\substack{p \in P \\ p \ne 2}} \frac{p}{p+1},
\]
the factor $\tfrac16$ being $2$'s own share---$v_2$ even and at least $2$---which
is why the product skips it. This is an upper bound for every $P$, not an
asymptotic, and it is all that is needed. Five of the twenty classes mod $33$ are
obstructed, so those primes have Dirichlet density $\tfrac14$ and $\sum 1/p$
diverges over them; letting $P$ grow drives the product to zero, and the
candidates have natural density $0$---candidates meaning what Corollary
\ref{cor:closed} defines, spindles the law admits whose shell is occupied. Whether
a rotation can join without a shell of its own is not settled here, so this is not
yet a statement that almost no spindle joins.

The decay is slow, which is why the first hundred look generous at $16$: measured,
the density of candidates up to $N$ runs $0.160$, $0.136$, $0.1236$, $0.1154$,
$0.1089$ at $N = 10^2, \dots, 10^6$. Those five are measurements of one thing; the
$0.10882$ they are converging on is a measurement of another---the right-hand side
of the bound above, with $P$ taken to be the obstructed primes below
$2\cdot10^6$---and the two agree at $N = 10^6$ only because the bound is nearly
tight there, not because one computes the other. A Mertens estimate over the obstructed primes would give the rate
$(\log N)^{-1/4}$, which the measurements fit; we do not prove it, and nothing
below uses it. Density zero is the claim, and it needs only the divergence---and
only the \emph{necessary} half of Theorem~\ref{prop:shells}: for any finite set
$P$ of obstructed primes the occupied-shell candidates have upper density at most
the local product over $P$, and letting $P$ grow sends that to $0$.

The local mechanism is worth seeing directly, since it is what
Theorem~\ref{prop:shells}'s proof turns into a norm condition. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:rank} the $\Q$-span of $\Lat$ is
$V = \Q(\sqrt{33})(\sqrt{-3})$, and $\lvert z\rvert^2 = z\bar z$ is the norm of
that quadratic extension, so a rational $a$ is a squared radius in $V$ exactly
when it is a norm from $V$ down to $\Q(\sqrt{33})$---that is, when $a = x^2+3y^2$
is soluble there. An obstructed $p$ splits in $\Q(\sqrt{33})$, so the local field
is $\Q_p$ itself and both local valuations are $v_p$; and $-3$ is a non-square
there, so the local extension is unramified quadratic. Its norm group is the
elements of even valuation, which is where the parity comes from. At an
\emph{inert} $p$ there is no condition at all, whatever $-3$ does: the local field
is then already the unramified quadratic extension of $\Q_p$, so it contains
$\sqrt{-3}$ and the norm map is onto. That is why the definition asks for both
symbols and not just the second. The passage from local solubility to a norm of an
algebraic \emph{integer} is where $h(V) = 1$ and the unit $\eta$ enter, and that is
the content of the proof above; Lemma~\ref{lem:auto} is what makes the chart and
$\Lat$ literally the same points at integer radii, so no index-$16$ correction
intervenes when one computes with coordinates. Independently of the proof, the
criterion was checked against the form for every $a \le 400$ and for targeted
larger values including $17^2, 17^3, 29^2, 41^2, 101^2$ and the powers of $2$ up to
$2^8$, with no exception.

Of the integer spindles to $a = 100$ this leaves the shells at $a = 8$, $24$,
$32$, $40$, $56$, $68$, $72$, $88$, $96$ empty, and the rest are candidates. The rational ones are constrained before any search: by
Lemma~\ref{lem:A} every rational squared norm of $\Lat$ lies in $\tfrac19\Z$, so a
rational shell radius $a$ has denominator $1$, $3$ or $9$ and nothing else. Every
normalised rational spindle with any other denominator spins an \emph{empty}
shell, in one line and with no computation---the denominator-$5$ family among
them. That is not a death: Theorem~\ref{thm:law} is silent at an empty shell, as
the remark after it says. What it does mean is that such a rotation has no pair
$p,\theta_a(p)$ of its own to work with, so nothing here supplies it a
construction either. Without the normalisation the sentence would be false:
$\theta_{4/15}$ has an empty shell and carries the mirror of the record.

\begin{lemma}[chaining a monochromatic pair]\label{lem:chain}
Let $M \subset \Lat$ be finite with two marked points $0$ and $t \ne 0$ such that
every proper $4$-colouring of $M$ gives them the same colour, and let $n \ge 1$ be
such that $a = n^2\lvert t\rvert^2$ is rational and exceeds $\tfrac14$, so that
$\theta_a$ is one of the rotations of Section~\ref{sec:intro}. Put
$M_n = \bigcup_{j=0}^{n-1}(M + jt)$. Then $M_n \subset \Lat$, every proper
$4$-colouring of $M_n$ gives $0$ and $nt$ the same colour, and
$\lvert M_n \rvert \le n(\lvert M \rvert - 1) + 1$. Consequently, writing
$a = n^2\lvert t \rvert^2$,
\[
  M_n \cup \theta_a(M_n)
\]
is not $4$-colourable, on at most $2n(\lvert M \rvert - 1) + 1$ vertices.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\Lat$ is a group and $t \in \Lat$, so every translate $M + jt$ lies in $\Lat$ and
so does the union; $a > \tfrac14$ makes $\sqrt{4a-1}$ real, so $\theta_a$ exists,
and $a \in \Q$ makes it a spindle. In a proper $4$-colouring the copy $M + jt$
forces $jt$ and
$(j+1)t$ to agree, so $0$ and $nt$ agree; the copies overlap in at least the
shared marked point, which gives the count. The same argument inside
$\theta_a(M_n)$ forces $\theta_a(0) = 0$ and $\theta_a(nt)$ to agree, and the two
copies share the origin, so $nt$ and $\theta_a(nt)$ both carry the colour of the
origin. But $\lvert nt \rvert^2 = a$ puts $nt$ on the shell, so
$\lvert nt - \theta_a(nt) \rvert = 1$ and they are adjacent.
\end{proof}

Parts' $367$-vertex gadget is such an $M$, with $t = 8/3$: every one of its
vertices lies in $\Lat$, and forcing its two terminals apart leaves it not
$4$-colourable, which is his monochromatic-pair property re-proved here rather
than cited. So $n = 1, 2, 3$ give joins at $a = 64/9$, $256/9$ and $64$, on at
most $733$, $1465$ and $2197$ vertices---the first two are the rational spindles
above, reached without any of this paper's machinery, and \emph{the third is
$a = 64$}, the one integer spindle Section~\ref{sec:stronger} cannot decide. It
is decided: it joins. The $421$-vertex gadget at $8/\sqrt3$ chains the same way
and gives $\alpha_{64/3}$ at $n = 1$.

What these constructions deliver is $\chi \ge 5$, which is not the same as
$\chi = 5$; the following disposes of the difference once, for all of them.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:subjoin}
Let $G = L \cup \theta(S)$ be a finite non-$4$-colourable join sharing the origin.
Then some induced subgraph of $G$ containing the origin is a join of the same
form and has chromatic number exactly $5$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Among the induced subgraphs of $G$ that contain the origin and are not
$4$-colourable---a non-empty family, since $G$ is one---take one, $H$, with
fewest vertices. Then $\chi(H) \ge 5$. For the other direction pick any
$v \ne 0$ of $H$; by minimality $H - v$ has a proper $4$-colouring, and giving
$v$ a fifth colour extends it to $H$, so $\chi(H) \le 5$. And $H$ is a join of the
same form: putting
\[
  L_H = L \cap V(H), \qquad S_H = \theta^{-1}\bigl(V(H) \cap \theta(S)\bigr),
\]
we have $V(H) = L_H \cup \theta(S_H)$ with $L_H \subseteq L$ and
$S_H \subseteq S$, and $0 \in V(H)$ by construction.
\end{proof}

\noindent So every entry of the table gives a $5$-chromatic join, of at most the
size listed; whether the \emph{full} union has chromatic number $5$ rather than
more is a separate question, settled here only where a proper $5$-colouring or
vertex-criticality has been checked.

\section{A stronger filter, and what survives it}\label{sec:stronger}

The criterion of Section \ref{sec:ball} uses group homomorphisms, which are
exactly the \emph{linear} proper colourings of $\Lat/m\Lat$. Every proper colouring
of that finite quotient pulls back to the whole lattice, linear or not, so using
all of them is strictly stronger, and it still needs no ball. A periodic colouring
gives a cross pair $(p,q)$ its colours through $p \bmod m\Lat$ and $q \bmod m\Lat$
alone, so all it can see is which \emph{residue pairs} occur; and $(\alpha,\beta)$
occurs exactly when the coset $(\alpha - \theta\beta) + m\Lam$ contains a unit
vector of the joint lattice.

One degeneracy must be excluded: if the pair $(0,0)$ occurs---equivalently if
$\Lam$ holds a vector of length $1/m$---then both halves give the centre's coset
the same colour and the test cannot kill. At $m = 2$ that condition is precisely
the half-unit criterion of Section \ref{sec:closed}, so everything Proposition
\ref{prop:half} disposes of is degenerate at $m = 2$; $m = 4$ and $m = 8$ are where it
discriminates.

Applied to the integer spindles to $a = 100$, it kills $\theta_a$ for
$a = 12$, $20$, $44$, $48$, $52$, $60$, $76$, $80$, $84$, $92$, $100$. The
modulus is not the same for all of them, and saying which matters, because a
rotation degenerate at one modulus is not thereby a survivor. Nine die at
$m = 4$, over $4^4 = 256$ residue classes. The remaining two, $a = 48$ and
$a = 80$, are degenerate at $m = 4$ \emph{and} at $m = 6$---the pair $(0,0)$
occurs at both---and die at $m = 8$, over $4096$ classes. Each kill is recorded as
a witness naming its modulus: the two colourings and every occurring residue pair,
re-checked to be proper, to agree at the shared origin, and to disagree on all
$69$ pairs. That leaves $\theta_4$,
$\theta_{16}$, $\theta_{28}$, $\theta_{36}$ --- and $\theta_{64}$, which is left
for the other reason: the pair $(0,0)$ occurs at every modulus we tried, $4$, $6$,
$8$ and $12$, so the filter never gets to speak. It never could have:
Lemma~\ref{lem:chain} exhibits a $5$-chromatic join at $a = 64$, and no colouring
argument excludes what exists. That
was forced rather than unlucky. $\Lat \cap \Q = \tfrac13\Z$, and the vector $(8/m)(1-\theta_{64})$ has
length $(8/m)\lvert 1-\theta_{64}\rvert = 1/m$ and lies in $\Lam_{64}$ as soon as
$8/m \in \tfrac13\Z$, which holds for every $m \mid 24$. (It has to be the unit
vector $1$ and not an arbitrary one: $\Lat$ is not a ring, and $\tfrac13\omega_3$
is outside it.) All
four moduli divide $24$. Moduli with $m \nmid 24$---$5$, $7$, $9$, $10$, $16$---
escape this particular degeneracy, though nothing says another does not await
there. The
cases $a = 12, 20, 44$ were
already known $4$-colourable by ball tests, which is the check on the method; the
other eight had never been tested, needing balls of radius $6.9$ to $10$. Each kill is a certificate, and a finite one: the two periodic colourings are
checked against \emph{every} residue pair that occurs, which by the criterion above
is every coset of $m\Lam$ holding a unit vector of the joint lattice. That is the
whole infinite union, not a sample of it. As corroboration the same pair is then
run against the exact edge set of a ball holding the whole shell---for
$\theta_{12}$, zero monochromatic edges of $867054$; for $\theta_{20}$, zero of
$2076192$---which tests the implementation rather than the claim.

So the divisibility law is necessary but far from sufficient. For the sixteen
occupied-shell \emph{integer} spindles that Theorem~\ref{thm:law} admits to
$a = 100$---the candidates as Section~\ref{sec:seven} defines them---the account
is now complete: eleven die to a test that uses every periodic colouring, four
are the constructions of Section~\ref{sec:seven}, and $a = 64$ joins by
Lemma~\ref{lem:chain}. The empty-shell multiples of $4$ lie outside that tally, since the law is silent
at an empty shell, and they are worth settling separately rather than leaving
implicit. There are nine of them below $100$, and both filters have now been run
on all nine. Six die: $a = 8$, $32$, $40$, $56$, $68$ and $96$ each carry a
homomorphism $4$-colouring---in fact $96$ of them---so no subset of the joint
lattice is 5-chromatic there either. Three survive both: $a = 24$, $72$ and $88$
admit no homomorphism $4$-colouring at all, and the periodic filter proves at
modulus $4$ that no pair of $4$-periodic colourings kills them, over the $66$
residue pairs that occur in each. Those three are genuine candidates, on the same
footing as the survivors above and with no construction known; they are not
counted in the sixteen, because the law never admitted them.

That the filters can speak here at all is worth a sentence. An empty shell means
no point is paired with its own image, so the spindle mechanism is absent---but
$\Lam_a$ still has rank $8$ and $72$ unit vectors, and the cross edges they give
pair \emph{distinct} points. An earlier version of the accompanying code declined
to run the periodic filter at an empty shell, on the ground that the question did
not arise. It does arise, and these three are what the refusal was hiding. Nothing here
covers the rational candidates---three of which work---or the finer lattices, or
rotations outside the spindle family, where Section~\ref{sec:notclaimed} reports
both tools blind.

\section{What every construction must contain}\label{sec:interface}

The law constrains the rotation. The same eight colourings constrain the
\emph{construction}, and in a way that does not depend on which rotation is used.

Let $G = L \cup \theta(S)$ with $L$ and $\theta(S)$ meeting only at the origin,
so the only edges between the halves are the cross edges of
Figure~\ref{fig:orbits}. Both halves sit in the same lattice $\Lat$, so both are
coloured by the eight homomorphisms counted in Section~\ref{sec:arith}---two
Klein, six cyclic---whatever $\theta$ is. Pair any colouring of $L$ with any colouring of $S$, permute the four
colours on the second, and the result properly $4$-colours $G$ \emph{unless} some
cross edge comes out monochromatic. A 5-chromatic $G$ must therefore carry a set
of cross edges meeting the monochromatic set of every admissible pairing: a
minimum hitting set, decided exactly.

Which pairings are admissible matters. The halves share the origin and a homomorphism sends it to
colour $0$ on both sides, so only the $6$ permutations fixing $0$ give a
well-defined colouring of $G$; the other $18$ colour the shared point twice over.
Counting all $24$ imposes a constraint no colouring realises and inflates the
bound.

No ball is needed for any of this, which is worth establishing before the count,
because it is what makes the bound a statement about the lattice.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:cross}
Suppose $\Lam_a = \Lat \oplus \theta_a(\Lat)$ has rank $8$. Then
$(p,\theta_a(q)) \mapsto p - \theta_a(q)$ is a bijection from the cross edges of
$\Lat \cup \theta_a(\Lat)$---pairs $p \in \Lat$ and $\theta_a(q) \in
\theta_a(\Lat)$ at distance $1$ from each other---onto the unit vectors of
$\Lam_a$. There are finitely many, though the union is dense. Moreover such an edge is monochromatic under a
homomorphism $\varphi$ of $\Lam_a$ exactly when $\varphi(u) = 0$, so what a cross
edge contributes depends only on $u$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $\lvert p - \theta_a(q)\rvert = 1$ then $u = p - \theta_a(q)$ is a unit vector
of $\Lam_a$. Conversely the direct sum writes a unit vector uniquely as
$u = r + \theta_a(s)$, and then $p = r$, $q = -s$ is the only pair with
$p - \theta_a(q) = u$. Writing $\varphi = (\varphi_1,\varphi_2)$ on the two
summands, $\varphi(u) = \varphi_1(p) - \varphi_2(\theta_a(q))$, which vanishes
exactly when the two ends receive the same colour.
\end{proof}

The unit vectors of $\Lam_a$ lie among the integer points of one ellipsoid and are
enumerated exactly from it, so the entire infinite union has a \emph{finite and
complete} cross-edge list. For $\theta_4$ it has $126$ entries, of which $60$ meet the
shared origin and $66$ do not, and the $8^2 \cdot 6 = 384$ admissible pairings
leave $235$ distinct monochromatic patterns, none empty. Measured against balls
of $5677$, $8131$, $10369$ and $14323$ points, the cross-edge count is $126$
every time, as Proposition~\ref{prop:cross} requires.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:five}
No 5-chromatic $L \cup \theta_4(S)$ has four or fewer cross edges, for any
$L, S \subseteq \Lat$ whatever: the hitting-set instance is unsatisfiable at $4$
and satisfiable at $5$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorems \ref{thm:five}--\ref{thm:profile}]
These three are computations, and what has to be computed is fixed by
Proposition~\ref{prop:cross} and the count of colourings above, with no ball and
no choices left. The objects are: the $126$ unit vectors of $\Lam_4$, of which
$60$ meet the shared origin and $66$ do not; the $8$ homomorphism $4$-colourings
of $\Lat$, two Klein and six cyclic; the $8^2 \cdot 6 = 384$ admissible pairings
of one colouring per half with a colour permutation fixing $0$; and the $235$
distinct non-empty monochromatic patterns those pairings cut out of the $66$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:five} is then the statement that the minimum set meeting all
$235$ has size $5$, Theorem~\ref{thm:1920} that there are $1920$ such sets, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:profile} the profile and factorisation of those $1920$.

Each step is finite and exact. \texttt{scripts/interface\_bound\_exact.py 4}
enumerates the unit vectors from the trace form, builds the $235$ patterns and
runs the minimum hitting set. That last step is an exhaustive search over the
$235$ patterns, not a solver call: no set of four or fewer meets them all, and a
set of five does. The search space is small enough to redo by hand in seconds,
and no part of Theorems~\ref{thm:five}--\ref{thm:profile} rests on an
unsatisfiability verdict that would need a certificate; \texttt{scripts/interface\_matchings.py} enumerates all optimal sets, checks
each is a perfect matching, and computes the profile and the
$30 \times 8 \times 8$ factorisation. Both print every intermediate count named
above, so a reader can check them one at a time rather than trusting a single
verdict. What the three theorems do \emph{not} say is that some 5-chromatic graph
realises five cross edges: the $1920$ defeat every pairing of homomorphism
colourings, which is necessary and not known to be sufficient.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:1920}
There are exactly $1920$ such five-edge sets. None meets the shared origin---an
edge out of it is never monochromatic, since a homomorphism sends the origin to
$0$ and no unit vector to $0$, so the $60$ cross edges at the centre appear in no
pattern at all. Every one of the $1920$ is a perfect matching, five edges on ten
vertices; that is inherited rather than earned, because the remaining $66$ cross
edges are themselves a perfect matching.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:profile}
In the taxonomy of Figure~\ref{fig:orbits} all $1920$ have one profile: exactly
one reference edge, both ends at radius $2$, and exactly four auxiliary edges at
radii $\tfrac{\sqrt{11}}{2}\mp\tfrac{\sqrt3}{6}$, two in each crosswise
direction. So the radius of all ten interface vertices is fixed before any search
begins. The count factors as
\[
  1920 = 30 \times 8 \times 8 :
\]
any of the $30$ reference edges, and then, \emph{for that reference edge}, one of
exactly $8$ admissible auxiliary pairs in each direction, every one of the $64$
combinations occurring. The $8$ depends on the reference edge---across all $30$
there are $72$ pairs per direction---so the three choices are not independent,
only the last two. Of the $\binom{66}{5} = 8936928$ five-subsets of the usable
cross edges, these $1920$ are $0.021\%$.
\end{theorem}

The bound is one-sided in the safe direction: the $235$ patterns come from
homomorphism colourings only, and any colouring of the halves that is not one
would add patterns and could only raise the bound. Five is a floor, not an
estimate. And by Proposition~\ref{prop:cross} it is a floor over the whole
lattice and not merely over a pool: there is no larger ball to try, since $126$
is all the cross edges $\Lat \cup \theta_4(\Lat)$ has.

Two things it does not do. It does not rank rotations by promise---$\theta_{16}$
has the weakest obstruction measurable here and its smallest known graph has
$2901$ vertices, while $\theta_{28}$ ties $\theta_4$ at five and its smallest has
$156577$ against $509$. Both are bounds over the lattice: Proposition
\ref{prop:cross} makes the whole computation ball-free, since the pattern a cross
edge contributes depends only on its unit vector, and run that way $\theta_4$,
$\theta_{16}$, $\theta_{28}$ and $\theta_{36}$ offer $66$, $66$, $120$ and $90$
usable cross edges with bounds $5$, $1$, $5$ and $5$. And it does not point at the record. Measured on Parts'
$509$-vertex graph itself, which is $L \cup \theta_4(S)$ with $\lvert L\rvert =
374$, $\lvert S\rvert = 136$ and exactly $18$ cross edges, all $384$ pairings are
non-empty---the strongest check the method gets, since an empty one would be a
proper $4$-colouring of a 5-chromatic graph---and \emph{six} of those $18$ edges
already defeat every closed-form colouring, themselves a perfect matching. Five is
not reachable inside the record's own interface: the $1920$ five-edge matchings
live among the ball's $126$ cross edges and the record carries only $18$ of them,
so five is a floor over the lattice and not a prediction about any particular
graph. The other twelve are there to defeat colourings that are not
homomorphisms, which is exactly what this argument cannot see.

\section{The same question on another lattice}\label{sec:hept}

Nothing in Section~\ref{sec:ball} is about the Moser lattice. The criterion needs
a finitely generated subgroup of $(\C,+)$, its unit vectors, and a rotation, so
the question can be asked wherever those exist. Haugland's Moser-spindle-free
graph \cite{Haugland} is the case that matters at the moment: de Grey has raised
it publicly as a possible route past $509$, and asked in particular about using
more than two of its sets of $42$ vectors \cite{Thread18}.

Which lattice, exactly. The construction lives in $\Q(\zeta_{420})$, of degree
$96$. Write $L_1$ for the subgroup of $(\C,+)$ generated by the $42$ vectors
$\zeta_{420}^{10j} = \zeta_{42}^{\,j}$---all the $42$nd roots of unity---and, for
$k > 1$,
\[
  L_k = \Big\langle\, \rho^{\,s}\zeta_{42}^{\,j} \;:\; 0 \le s < k,\ 0 \le j < 42
  \,\Big\rangle,
  \qquad
  \rho = \frac{-\sqrt3 + i\,(4\cos\tfrac\pi7 - 1)}{4\sin\tfrac{2\pi}7}\,
         \zeta_3^{-1},
\]
the multiplier that carries one set of $42$ to the next. The formula is read off
Haugland's own parametrisation \cite{Haugland}, in which the second set of $42$ is
the first rotated by the argument of $(-\sqrt3,\ 4\cos\tfrac\pi7 - 1)$; that
vector has length exactly $4\sin\tfrac{2\pi}7$, which is what makes the displayed
quotient an algebraic number of modulus exactly $1$, and the factor
$\zeta_3^{-1}$ aligns his indexing of the $42$ directions with $\zeta_{42}^j$. It
reproduces the offset he reports, $0.42363201413287$, to fourteen places. That
number is $\arg\rho \cdot 42/2\pi$, the argument measured in units of the $42$-fold
step, not an angle: $\arg\rho$ itself is $3.63113154971030^\circ$. Read as radians
or as degrees it would say this rotation is the wrong one. So $\rho$
lies in $\Q(\zeta_{420})$, has modulus exactly $1$, and is not a root of unity:
its argument is $3.6311^\circ$. Which of these lattices de Grey's question and
Haugland's own graph concern is a matter for \cite{Thread18} and \cite{Haugland};
what is defined here is $L_k$, and every claim below is about one of them.
\emph{Everything reported below about individual rotations---the table, the
survival of $\theta_2$, the unit and half-unit counts---is computed in $L_1$}, and
none of it is claimed for $L_k$ with $k > 1$ or for whichever of these Haugland's
own graph uses; the reader should take his paper for that. What is computed for
several $k$ is only the rank and the covolume, and it is labelled where it
appears.

Degree $96$ looks prohibitive and is not: the $\Q$-span of $L_1$ is
$\Q(\zeta_{42})$, of degree $\varphi(42) = 12$. More sets do not raise it---$L_1$
to $L_4$ all have rank $12$---and what they change is the covolume, which falls by
a factor of $7$ each time, so de Grey's suggestion is not cosmetic. A
twelve-dimensional Fincke--Pohst enumeration is routine, and the criterion ports.

The span being a field again, Proposition~\ref{prop:rank}'s argument transfers
verbatim, and settles the rank here too.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:heptrank}
$L_1 + \theta_N(L_1)$ has rank $12$ if $\theta_N \in \Q(\zeta_{42})$ and rank $24$
otherwise, with no intermediate value; and
\[
  \theta_N \in \Q(\zeta_{42}) \iff 4N-1 = 3k^2 \text{ or } 7k^2 .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The rank dichotomy is the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:rank} with
$\Q(\zeta_{42})$ in place of $V$: a non-zero element of the intersection would
express $\theta_N$ as a quotient of two field elements. For the criterion,
$\theta_N = c + is$ with $c$ rational, so $\theta_N \in \Q(\zeta_{42})$ iff
$\sqrt{-(4N-1)} \in \Q(\zeta_{42})$. Since $42 = 2\cdot 21$ with $21$ odd,
$\Q(\zeta_{42}) = \Q(\zeta_{21})$, whose Galois group
$(\Z/21)^{\times} \cong \Z/2 \times \Z/6$ has exactly three subgroups of index
$2$; the three quadratic subfields are therefore $\Q(\sqrt{-3})$,
$\Q(\sqrt{-7})$ and $\Q(\sqrt{21})$. A square root of a negative number rules out
the real one, leaving $-(4N-1) = -3k^2$ or $-7k^2$.
\end{proof}

Measured against the direct rank computation: $N = 1, 2, 7, 16$ give rank $12$
and $N = 4, 9$ give rank $24$, exactly as the criterion says. It also explains
$\theta_4$, Haugland's own rotation: $4\cdot 4 - 1 = 15$ is neither $3k^2$ nor
$7k^2$, so its join is a genuine rank-$24$ object and not a symmetry.

Which spindles exist there at all is settled first, and arithmetically:
$\theta_N$ needs $\sqrt{4N-1}$ in the field, and $\Q(\zeta_{420})$ contains
$\sqrt m$ exactly for the eight squarefree divisors $m$ of $105$. Of $N \le 16$
that leaves $N = 1, 2, 4, 7, 9, 16$; every other $N$ is excluded before any
computation.

Then the criterion has a blind spot, and it is worth stating in general because
it is not special to this lattice.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:free}
Let $\Lam$ be any subgroup of $(\C,+)$ closed under $\omega = e^{i\pi/3}$ and
containing a unit vector $g$, and let $a = 4m^2$ for a positive integer $m$. Then
$\Lam + \theta_a(\Lam)$ contains a vector of length $\tfrac12$, so by
Proposition~\ref{prop:half} the criterion cannot kill $\theta_a$: neither group
of order $4$ admits a homomorphism avoiding the unit vectors. Both lattices here
are $\omega$-closed---$\Lat$ by construction, and Haugland's because $\omega$ is
one of its $42$ roots of unity.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\lvert 2mg\rvert = 2m = \sqrt a$, so $2mg$ lies on the shell, and
\[
  h_g \;=\; \tfrac12\bigl(2mg - \theta_a(2mg)\bigr) \;=\; m(1-\theta_a)g
\]
lies in $\Lam + \theta_a(\Lam)$ with length $m \lvert 1-\theta_a\rvert =
m/\sqrt a = \tfrac12$. Proposition~\ref{prop:half} does the rest, both halves of
it, since $\Lam$ is $\omega$-closed.
\end{proof}

Haugland joins by $\theta_4$, whose shell radius is $2$, so
Proposition~\ref{prop:free} applies and the criterion says nothing there---not a
failed computation but a proof that this particular test is silent. Measured and
consistent: the joint lattice $L_1 + \theta_4(L_1)$ has rank $24$ and $210$ unit
vectors, of which exactly $42$ lie in $2\bigl(L_1 + \theta_4(L_1)\bigr)$, one per
generator, as the proposition predicts.

The useful consequence is that the test has content precisely at the rotations
Proposition~\ref{prop:free} misses, and the classification above supplies them. In
$L_1$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llllll}
\hline
$N$ & shell & $\sqrt{4N-1}$ & units & half-unit vectors & verdict \\
\hline
$1$ & $42$ & $\sqrt3$ & $42$ & $0$ & dead, by a Klein colouring \\
$2$ & $84$ & $\sqrt7$ & $84$ & $0$ & \textbf{survives} \\
$4$ & --- & $\sqrt{15}$ & $210$ & $42$ & survives by Proposition~\ref{prop:free} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

So $\theta_2$ is the one non-trivial survivor found: it has no half-unit vector,
so it gets no free pass, and a complete certificate search---affordable at rank
$12$, where this lattice's own rank-$24$ joins are not---returns nothing. And
$\theta_2$ is a rotation nobody has been able to use, because it needs $\sqrt7$,
which the classical field $\Q(\sqrt3,\sqrt5,\sqrt{11})$ does not contain. Here it
is available and it passes a test with content.

Of the remaining three, $N = 16$ needs no computation at all: its shell radius is
$4$, so $a = 4m^2$ with $m = 2$ and Proposition~\ref{prop:free} gives it the same
free pass as $\theta_4$. That leaves $N = 7$ and $N = 9$, and we stop there
honestly---but we can now say what stops us, which is not what it looked like.
It is not the dimension. By Proposition~\ref{prop:heptrank}, $4\cdot7-1 = 27 =
3\cdot 3^2$, so $\theta_7$ stays at rank $12$, the same as the cases that finish
in a second. What differs is the denominator: the Fincke--Pohst target is
$96N\,\mathrm{den}^2$, and the joint lattice's denominator is $1$ at $N = 1$, $2$
at $N = 2$ and $7$ at $N = 7$, so $\theta_7$ searches a target $343$ times
$\theta_1$'s where $\theta_2$'s is $8$ times it. It did not finish in ninety
minutes. ($N = 9$ is rank $24$ as well, so it is harder on both
counts.) Reaching either needs the shell and the unit vectors described
arithmetically rather than enumerated---the cyclotomic analogue of the chart
\eqref{eq:chart}, which exists but which we have not written. Nor is any of this a construction:
$\theta_2$ survives, and surviving is being a candidate.

\section{What is not claimed}\label{sec:notclaimed}

The criterion is a one-sided kill. A survivor is a candidate; only an explicit
5-chromatic union makes it a construction, which is why Section \ref{sec:seven}
exhibits graphs rather than citing survivals.

The law is about \emph{this} lattice, and Section~\ref{sec:hept} is the evidence:
$\theta_2$ dies on the Moser lattice and survives on $L_1$, the lattice generated
by the $42$nd roots of unity. The divisibility
law does not transfer, and no attempt is made to transfer it.

The rank-$8$ hypothesis excludes rotations that map $\Lat$'s span into
itself---$\theta_3$ is $\omega_3$, $\theta_1$ is $\omega_1$---where the cross
decomposition does not arise. Proposition~\ref{prop:rank} says exactly which
those are, $4a-1 = 3k^2$ or $11k^2$, so the hypothesis is recognisable at sight
rather than checkable per rotation, and it is proved rather than assumed.

Proposition \ref{prop:half} is unconditional for both groups of order $4$. An
earlier version of it was not, and the remark in Section~\ref{sec:closed} records
why: the three exceptions it reported were a bug in the sweep, not a feature of
the lattice.

Theorem~\ref{prop:shells} is proved, through
Proposition~\ref{prop:order}: $\Lat$ is $\tfrac13\Ok_V$, so occupancy is a
question about relative norms and Hasse's theorem answers it. An earlier version
of this paper stated it as verified and not proved, having missed that $3\Lat$ is
the maximal order. Theorem~\ref{thm:law} does
not---it is proved---and the constructions of Section~\ref{sec:seven} are
exhibited rather than predicted, so nothing in Section~\ref{sec:seven} rests on
the criterion either.

The choice of $\Lat$ is a choice. Taking $\lvert j\rvert \le J$ instead of
$\lvert j\rvert \le 2$ in the generating family gives, for each $J$, a rank-$4$
lattice, and these nest: $30 \subset 42 \subset 54 \subset \cdots$. The index over
ours is $9^{J-2}$, checked for $J \le 5$, and the unit vectors are exactly the
$6(2J+1)$ generators, checked for $J \le 3$. Neither is proved here for all $J$;
counting generators does not by itself rule out a further unit vector, and nothing
below depends on the general statement.
Heule's $553$-vertex large part lives in the $42$-vector member. Everything here
is about the $30$-vector one, which is what the record and every construction in
Section \ref{sec:seven} use; the law is not claimed for the finer members, and
the criterion would have to be recomputed on each.

Nothing here reaches the rotations of \cite{Voronov} that are not spindles, and
we checked rather than assumed it. Their four are $\rho u$ for a unit vector $u$
of $\Lat$; the whole family $\{\rho u\}$ is $30$ rotations, of which six---the
sixth roots of unity---fix $\Lat$ and so give back $\theta_4$'s own joint lattice,
and exactly two are spindles, $\rho$ and $-\rho$. Every one of the $30$ has a
rank-$8$ joint lattice with $126$, $108$ or $90$ unit vectors. Run against all
$30$, the criterion of Section \ref{sec:ball} kills \emph{none}, and the stronger
periodic filter of Section \ref{sec:stronger} kills none either, degenerating at
$m = 4$ and $m = 8$ alike. So both tools are blind outside the spindle family:
against the integer spindles the law removes three $a$ in four and the periodic
filter eleven of the sixteen survivors, and against $\{\rho u\}$ they remove
nothing at all. Whatever decides that family is not in this paper.

No claim is made about the record. The bound $509$ is unbeaten, and nothing here
brings it closer. What is settled is an obstruction---no spindle of rank $8$ with
$4 \nmid a$ can be used at all, the rank-$4$ exceptions of
Proposition~\ref{prop:rank} being untouched---together with a proved criterion for
occupancy,
eleven further exclusions and seven constructions. What is not settled is the
classification those would have to add up to: rotations
outside the spindle family are untouched.

\section{The odd case, and the sweep}\label{app:odd}

The trichotomy at the end of Step 4 settles the odd case together with the even
one, for every $\lambda$ at once. This appendix records the finer analysis it
replaced---which says not merely that each $\lambda \ne -1$ is harmless but which
$\lambda$ occur---and the computational sweep that checks both against every
rank-$8$ spindle to $a = 80$. Nothing in the paper depends on either.

\subsection*{Which $\lambda$ occur at odd $a$}
For odd $a$ Lemma \ref{lem:E} says nothing, and two sub-cases exhaust what occurs.

\emph{(a) $\lambda$ rational.} Write $\lambda = \alpha/\beta$ with
$\alpha, \beta$ coprime integers, $\beta \ge 1$. A rational $\lambda$ makes
$m = \lvert v \rvert^2$ rational, so $m$ is a rational squared norm of a non-zero
point of $\Lat$, hence $m \ge 1/9$ by Section \ref{sec:arith}. Rearranged,
\eqref{eq:star} reads $m\big[a(\alpha+\beta)^2 - \alpha\beta\big] = a$. Both
signs end up bounding the same quantity, $\lvert\alpha\rvert - \lvert\beta\rvert$,
but for different reasons.

\emph{Negative $\lambda$}, $\lambda = -p/q$ with $p,q \ge 1$, $d = q-p$. Here
\eqref{eq:star} reads $a d^2 + pq = a/m$. With $pq \ge 1$,
\[
  d^2 < 1/m \le 9, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \lvert d \rvert \le 2 ,
\]
and $d = 0$ is $\lambda = -1$; $\lvert d \rvert = 3$ is impossible, not merely
unobserved. If $\lvert d \rvert = 1$ then exactly one of $p,q$ is even, so $2$ and
hence both primes divide one of $r,s$, while the other is an odd multiple of $v$
and Corollary \ref{cor:even} keeps its colour non-zero: $\varphi(u) = 0 + c$ with
$c \ne 0$ once more. (Here $a$ is odd, so it is the first half of the corollary
that applies, not the second.) If $\lvert d \rvert = 2$ then $p$ and $q$ are coprime and differ by $2$, so
both are odd; $4a + pq = a/m$ forces $m < 1/4$, and $1/9$ is the only squared
norm below $1/4$ (Section \ref{sec:arith}), so $m = 1/9$ exactly and
$pq = 5a$. Then $r = qv$ and $s = -pv$ with $\lvert v \rvert^2 = 1/9$ and $p,q$
odd, so $\pi_i$ divides neither, multiplication by them is invertible mod $\pi_i$,
and $\gamma_i(r) = 0 \iff \pi_i \mid v \iff \gamma_i(s) = 0$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:F}
In that case $\pi_i$ does not divide $v$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $v = \pi w$ with $w \in \Lat$ then, $\pi$ being real,
$\lvert v \rvert^2 = \pi^2 \lvert w \rvert^2$, so
$\lvert w \rvert^2 = 1/(9\pi^2) = (58 \mp 10\sqrt{33})/144$ and \eqref{eq:norm}
demands $A^2 + 33B^2 + 3C^2 + 11D^2 = 58$. The congruences force
$A \equiv B \equiv C \equiv D \pmod 2$: all even makes the form $\equiv 0 \pmod 4$
against $58 \equiv 2$; all odd makes every square $\equiv 1 \pmod 8$, so the form
is $\equiv 1 + 33 + 3 + 11 = 48 \equiv 0 \pmod 8$, again against $58 \equiv 2$.
Neither is possible.
\end{proof}

So both colours are equal and non-zero, and the vector imposes the same diagonal
condition $(c,c)$ that the shell displacements of Step 2 impose. Nothing new.
(Two shorter routes reach the same place. Corollary \ref{cor:even} gives
$c \ne 0$ at once, since $c = 0$ would put $v$ in $\pi_i\Lat$; and $c = 0$ would put
$v$ in $2\Lat$, forcing $\lvert v/2 \rvert^2 = 1/36$, which Section
\ref{sec:arith} records as unattained---congruence (ii) refuses its only candidate,
$(\pm2,0,0,0)$. That second route is the one the accompanying code takes.
Lemma \ref{lem:F} needs neither, and proves the stronger statement that neither
prime divides $v$.)

\emph{Positive $\lambda$}, $\alpha,\beta \ge 1$. This sign occurs and is not
covered by the bound above: $\alpha\beta \ge 1$ now, so the rearrangement gives
$a\big[m(\alpha+\beta)^2 - 1\big] = m\alpha\beta$, and $4a > 1$---which
$\sqrt{4a-1}$ needs---is exactly $m(\alpha-\beta)^2 < 1$, so $(\alpha-\beta)^2 < 9$
again. The dichotomy is parity, not size. If $\alpha,\beta$ have opposite parity,
one is even, both primes divide the corresponding one of $r,s$, and the other
colour is again non-zero by Corollary \ref{cor:even}. If both are odd then
$\gamma_i(r) = \gamma_i(s) = \gamma_i(v)$ and the pair is the diagonal $(c,c)$ of
Step 2, with $c \ne 0$ by Corollary \ref{cor:even}. Nothing new either way. The
case is not vacuous: $a = 7/19$ is odd and of rank $8$, and
$v = \tfrac56 + \tfrac{i\sqrt3}{6}$, of squared norm $7/9$, gives a cross unit
vector with $\lambda = 1$.

\emph{(b) $\lambda$ irrational.} Then $\lvert v \rvert^2$ is irrational and the
bound of (a) is unavailable; the three cases above need none of it. For the record,
irrational $\lambda$ occurs only at even $a$ in every case computed
($a = 4, 10, 16, 28$, over all rank-$8$ spindles to $a = 80$), but nothing rests on
that now: it was the last computed-only step in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:law}, and the trichotomy removes it.

\subsection*{What the sweep checks}
The accompanying code checks Step 4 and the analysis above against the actual
cross unit vectors of every rank-$8$ spindle to $a = 80$, with zero violations,
and both halves of
Corollary \ref{cor:even} on all $552$ cross vectors with $\lambda \ne -1$ to
$a = 60$. Each prime divides $r$ or $s$ in every one of them---not only at even
$a$, where the corollary asks for it, but at odd $a$ as well---with exactly two
exceptions, $a = 39$ and $a = 51$. Both are the $\lvert d \rvert = 2$ case above,
where $p$ and $q$ are odd and neither $r$ nor $s$ is divisible; there Lemma \ref{lem:F}, or the trichotomy, applies instead. The trichotomy is checked
on the same $552$ directly: where $4 \nmid a$, no prime divides \emph{both} $r$
and $s$, again with zero counterexamples.

\begin{remark}
An earlier form of this analysis asserted that every rational $\lambda$ is a ratio of
\emph{consecutive} integers, $\lvert p - q \rvert = 1$, measured over every
rank-$8$ spindle with $a \le 35$. That is false: at $a = 39$ the cross vectors have
$\lambda = -13/15$ and $-15/13$. The measurement had stopped four short of its own
counterexample. The bound $\lvert d \rvert \le 2$ above is a proof, and
$\lvert d \rvert = 2$ occurs exactly when $pq = 5a$ with $m = 1/9$---which to
$a = 80$ means $a = 39$ and $a = 51$, the two the sweep singles out, since
$a = p(p+2)/5$ gives rank $4$ at $p = 3$ and $5$ and lands past $80$ at $p = 23$.
The positive-$\lambda$ branch refutes the old assertion a second time, from the other
side: $\lambda = 1$ has $\lvert \alpha - \beta \rvert = 0$.
\end{remark}

\section*{Code and data}

Every computation quoted here is carried out by a script in the accompanying
repository, and \texttt{paper/README.md} there maps each claim in this paper to
the script that reproduces it; \texttt{scripts/verify\_all.sh} runs all of them in
one pass. The archive contains, among the rest: the exact coordinates of all
seven constructions, each file naming the field it was written in on its first
line, together with the edge sets of the minimised graphs and the scripts that
recompute every edge and re-prove non-4-colourability from the coordinates in a
fresh process. One proof certificate is shipped, and it is not for our
constructions: \texttt{proofs/} holds a DRAT proof of the unsatisfiability of the
$4$-colouring instance of Parts' $509$-vertex record, with the checker's log
(\texttt{s VERIFIED}, $126$\,s). The seven unions of
Section~\ref{sec:seven} have no certificates and are labelled so in their own
table; the $66$ usable cross edges of $\theta_4$, the $235$
monochromatic patterns and the hitting-set instance behind Theorems
\ref{thm:five}--\ref{thm:profile}; the two periodic colourings certifying each of the eleven exclusions of Section
\ref{sec:stronger}, together with the complete list of occurring residue pairs
they are checked against---the global test, not the corroborating ball run---and
\texttt{scripts/audit\_numbers.py}, which re-derives the paper's numbers from the
definitions, sharing no code with the rest. One block of it used to be an
exception, reading the empty-shell colouring status from the repository's own
classification output rather than recomputing it---and that was the one block
deciding which of the nine empty-shell multiples of $4$ are open, so the hole sat
exactly where it mattered. \texttt{scripts/empty\_shell\_status.py} now derives all
nine from the definitions, running filter~1 on each joint lattice directly, and
the audit reads nothing.

The repository is
\url{https://github.com/calinnedelcu/joining-rotations-artifacts}, and the
snapshot accompanying this draft is the release tagged \texttt{v1.0} there; the
archived DOI below pins it immutably. Its \texttt{README.md}
opens with a coverage table saying which of this paper's claims the archive
settles and which it does not---among the latter, no proof certificates are
shipped and $\chi = 5$ is not established for any full union---and
\texttt{PROVENANCE.md} identifies the four files taken from \cite{Parts} rather
than produced by these scripts.
